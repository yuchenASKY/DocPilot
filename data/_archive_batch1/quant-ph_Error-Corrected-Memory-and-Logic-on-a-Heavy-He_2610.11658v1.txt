Large quantum computations require high-fidelity error-corrected memory and fault-tolerant log-
ical operations. To achieve these with superconducting circuits, quantum processors will need many
components operating with low error rates. In practice, fabrication of monolithic superconducting
devices produces unavoidable heterogeneity in component performance, which the quantum com-
puter must accommodate. We implement strategies to operate a large dynamic compass code on
an array of 156 superconducting qubits arranged on a heavy-hexagonal lattice, making use of the
entire monolithic quantum processor and redesigning our measurement schedule to exclude under-
performing components. We demonstrate improvements of up to 35% in logical error probabilities
for a memory experiment by using a noise-informed decoder combined with strategies to exclude
the worst-performing qubits and couplers, along with post-selecting on shots with a low probability
of leakage errors. In addition to these memory results, we also observe performance gains of up
to 56% when these strategies are applied to measurement-based logic, demonstrated by stability
experiments. We furthermore demonstrate high-fidelity fault-tolerant logic through lattice surgery.
Throughpre-selection filtering techniques, we can obtain Bell fidelities above 77%, and further
logical gap post-selection using exclusive decoding increases these fidelities to above 97%. Our ex-
periments demonstrate that high performance can be maintained in key elements of fault-tolerant
quantum memory and logic when working on monolithic superconducting quantum processors with
underperforming components, by using a suite of noise-informed quantum error correction strategies.
I. INTRODUCTION
Superconducting platforms offer scalable routes to-
wards fault-tolerant quantum computing. They ad-
mit short error-correcting cycle times resulting from
fast operations [1], high-yield device fabrication and
advanced characterization techniques [2–4], long coher-
ence times [5–7], high-fidelity two-qubit gates [7, 8],
and leakage mitigation strategies [9–13]. Key primi-
tives for fault-tolerant quantum computing have been
demonstrated [1, 14–24], and theoretical work has out-
lined pathways to achieving utility with superconducting
quantum devices [25–27].
To operate quantum computers at scale, we must per-
form high-fidelity error-correction and fault-tolerant logic
across large sections of devices. Superconducting plat-
forms pose several challenges in this regard. First, one
must design a code and architecture that can be operated
in a scalable fashion given a fixed connectivity. Second,
we must accurately decode errors, requiring us to obtain
good noise-characterization data and measurement infor-
mation in a timely and efficient manner. Third, any ap-
proach must allow for some number of defective or under-
performing components. Without this, superconducting
∗ stephen.bartlett@sydney.edu.au
devices may face prohibitively low manufacturing yields,
or the codes’ performance will suffer when operating on
large-scale chips [18]. Finally, scaling up to larger codes
introduces more points at which qubits may leak out of
the computational subspace, which can dramatically de-
teriorate performance [28].
In this work we demonstrate the operation of key el-
ements of error-correction and logic across the entirety
of a 156-qubit superconducting quantum computing de-
vice with a heavy-hex connectivity,ibm boston, finding
substantial performance gains by using noise-informed
approaches. We benchmark our error-correction perfor-
mance through quantum memory experiments, in which
logical qubits in a variety of stabilizer states are prepared,
and then measured after several rounds of syndrome ex-
traction. Memory experiments help determine the ability
of our error-correcting scheme to protect quantum infor-
mation while it is idling in a processor. We then bench-
mark fault-tolerant logic performance through stability
experiments [19, 29, 30], which characterize the ability
to perform logical measurements and manipulate codes,
critical for operations such as lattice surgery [31–34] and
logical Clifford gates [33, 35, 36]. Finally, we perform lat-
tice surgery experiments directly [16, 20, 22, 31–33, 37–
44] to measure the performance of fault-tolerant logic
in the device. These are sensitive to logical errors in
both memory and stability. These logical characteriza-

Our code of choice is the dynamic compass code [30,
45]. It is designed for the heavy-hex qubit array [46] and
has a relatively low footprint for a given code distance,
in comparison to other codes one might consider on this
qubit geometry [11, 47–53]. The dynamic compass code
also demonstrates a threshold in both memory and sta-
bility [30], meaning theoretically that it can be scaled to
improve performance in memory and logic.
We demonstrate the benefits of noise-informed decod-
ing in memory experiments operated over the entire de-
vice, as well as the utility of this approach in decoding
stability and lattice surgery experiments. We use aver-
aged circuit eigenvalue sampling (ACES) [54–56] to ex-
tract circuit-specific Pauli noise models that inform de-
coding. We can obtain more information by extracting
data from measurement pulses beyond the binary out-
comes, and using this data for soft decoding [57–59]. We
show that soft information and ACES noise data interact
in stability performance in ways not previously observed.
In particular, soft information improves the decoding of
stability experiments only when ACES information is not
used, bringing performance with a decoder informed by
a simpler noise model on par with ACES-informed de-
coding.
As larger superconducting chips are manufactured, a
degree of heterogeneity in the performance of components
such as couplers and qubits, and even completely defec-
tive components, will need to be tolerated in order to
maintain acceptable yields from the manufacturing pro-
cesses. The quantum processoribm boston, for example,
typically displays very low median error rates, but has a
small number of qubits or couplers suffering from noise
1 – 2 orders of magnitude higher [60]. We therefore seek
quantum error correction strategies that can maintain
high performance despite this heterogeneity. There has
been considerable recent effort towards designing defect-
exclusion strategies for the surface code [61–73] followed
by some demonstrations in experiment [18, 74]. However,
comparatively little work has been devoted to similar
strategies for codes on layouts with more general connec-
tivities, and in particular, lattices that are less connected
than a square grid, making defect avoidance more chal-
lenging [70, 72, 75]. Here, we design and implement an
approach for defect-mitigation in the dynamic compass
code operated on the heavy-hex qubit array. Our strate-
gies improve performance substantially in both memory
and stability inibm boston, beyond what is achievable
with noise-informed decoding alone.
Measurement data also provides information about
when leakage has occurred [18, 45, 76, 77]. We can obtain
estimates for the role of leakage in limiting performance
by post-selecting on runs in which it is likely that no
qubits have leaked. For our lattice surgery results, we
introduce a technique for leakagepre-selection [78], in
which we do not execute runs when the starting state
is likely to have already experienced some leakage. We
also investigate the effect of pre- and post-selection more
broadly. We find substantial improvements from remov-
ing shots that prepared an initial code state with error,
and also from using logical gap post-selection [79–82]. Fi-
nally, we observe that post-selecting on the convergence
of a belief propagation stage of the decoder [83] results
in similar improvements in a different experiment.
A. Summary of Results
We here present a summary of our results, presented in
detail in Section II. We introduce defect exclusion tech-
niques for the dynamic compass code in Section II A.
When combining ACES-informed BeliefMatching decod-
ing, defect mitigation and leakage post-selection, in full-
device memory experiments, we observe 4.11% and 2.56%
logical error probabilities per round in theZandXbases,
respectively. These are 32.3% and 35.2% improvements
compared to baseline performance, in the two bases, re-
spectively (Section II B). These results were qualitatively
consistent across multiple days of data collection. Our
best results, without any post-selection, found 4.4% and
2.9% logical error per round in the two bases, respec-
tively. On a different day in which the noisy components
were performing worse, we observed improvements result-
ing just from defect exclusion of up to 40%, depending
on the decoder used. We observe that defect exclusion
still produces sizeable gains even with good noise char-
acterization. We find that our 7×7 memory patch with
defect-mitigation meets or surpasses the average 5×5
memory patch performance in both bases, indicating a
key role for defect exclusion strategies in pushing mono-
lithic devices into the sub-threshold regime.
In stability, we observe continual error-suppression up
to 30 rounds of syndrome extraction only when we ex-
clude defects, whereas we find plateaus in patches with-
out defect mitigation starting around 12 rounds. At 30
rounds, we find an overall improvement of 56% in fail-
ure probability from our techniques (Section II C). We
observe that soft information improves performance only
when using reported (not ACES) noise for decoding.
When performing lattice surgery, we obtain lower
bounds on Bell fidelities of 77.3% when just using pre-
selection filters. When combining this with logical gap
post-selection, we obtain a Bell fidelity above 97.6% with
a post-selection yield of 27% (Section II D). In the Ap-
pendices, we report further results for memory, stability
and lattice surgery (including logical state teleportation)
experiments.

II. RESULTS
A. Dynamic Compass Code for a Heavy-Hex Qubit
Array Excluding Defective Components
We perform experiments using the IBM Quantum
Heronclass of superconducting quantum processor,
where 156 qubits are arranged on a plane with connectiv-
ity described by a heavy-hex lattice, displayed in Fig. 1.
We present results on the deviceibm boston, which sup-
ports mid-circuit measurements and high fidelity (me-
dian≃99.7%) two-qubit gates. The fidelity of processor
components vary day-to-day, but underperforming com-
ponents tend to cluster around one valence-2 qubit at site
46, and one valence-3 qubit at site 85 (see Sec. IV B for
discussion of defective qubits and couplers in the device).
We run experiments with the dynamic compass
code [30, 45], and test strategies to treat the defective
components. This code is compatible with the heavy-
hex qubit array and demonstrates a threshold in both
logical bases [30]. The code is a dynamic, or Floquet,
code withX-checks (Z-checks) consisting of four-body
(two-body) measurements on data qubits. The code is
designed from the gauge measurements of the heavy-hex
code [19, 46] for use on the heavy-hex layout. We con-
sider a 7×7 code for the memory experiment as shown
in Fig. 1, and a 5×5 code for the stability experiment as
shown in Fig. 5. The 7×7 memory patch is the largest
n×npatch that fits on the device. Both patches are large
enough that they cannot avoid operating across regions
with defective components.
Our sequence for the 7×7 code with defect exclu-
sion is shown in Fig. 1(b). Importantly, the excluded
qubits (black dots) and their neighboring couplers are
never used. Instead, we redesign gauge measurements
around the defective qubits to form superstabilizers that
are not supported on the defective components. This re-
sults in someXgauge checks being reduced to weight-2
or 3, and someZ-gauge checks reduced to weight-1.
Additionally, the dynamic nature of the code allows
for all stabilizers to remain low weight. The dynamic
compass code [30, 45] does not maintain a fixed stabilizer
group; we measure a sequence of anti-commuting checks
that changes the instantaneous stabilizer group over a
finite period [49, 84]. The four-step periodic sequence of
check measurements is shown in Fig. 1(b). In Steps 1
and 3 we measure allX-checks in the code. In Step 2
we measure a subset of theZ-checks and in Step 4 we

The IQ plane data is obtained from measurements,
which carry more than just binary classification infor-
mation. To perform leakage post-selection, we first pre-
pare and measure all qubits in states|0⟩,|1⟩and|2⟩,
and fit the resulting IQ data spreads to weighted Gaus-

can use the IQ data from QEC experiments to determine
the probability that each measurement detects a leakage
event. We discard any shots from the analysis in which
we find a probability of≥90% that the qubit has leaked.
Furthermore, for the shots that are not discarded, we use
the IQ data to produce probabilitiesp 0 andp 1 that the
qubit is in state|0⟩or state|1⟩, respectively, and take the
measurement outcome to be argmaxi (pi). The use of IQ
data and post-selection results in a further reduction in
logical error per round of 11.8% and 21.0% inZandX
bases, respectively (see Fig. 2). For all data, we use a
BeliefMatching decoder [83].

We also observe that the benefit of excluding defects is
retained even when supplying the decoder with very ac-

vide further data used to generate Fig. 3 in Appendix A.

cluding a non-defective component in the same patch

C. Stability Experiments and Fault-Tolerant Logic
We quantify the expected performance of fault-tolerant
logic gates by performing stability experiments with the
dynamic compass code onibm boston[19, 29, 30]. We
construct a 5×5-sized stability patch that covers a large
proportion of the device, see Fig. 5(a) for placement. We
cannot fit a stability patch of the same size as the memory
patch, owing to the additional row ofX-measure qubits
required, so we choose a 5×5 stability patch.
In our stability experiment, the boundary conditions
are determined such that the product of allX-checks in
a given time step is equal to the identity. Hence, in the
absence of noise, the product of allX-check measurement
outcomes at each round of the circuit is +1. If, after de-
coding overNmeasurement rounds, this observable reads
−1, we report a logical failure. Measurement-based gates
such as lattice surgery require a product of stabilizers to
be accurately measured. Stability experiments therefore
help to quantify the ability of the device to perform lat-
tice surgery operations, as the observable being tested
is a product of stabilizers. The distance of a stability
experiment is independent of the size of the patch. Con-
versely, we expect the circuit distance to scale linearly

III. DISCUSSION
In this work, we have demonstrated key elements of
quantum error correction and fault tolerance with the
dynamic compass code on a heavy-hex lattice of 156
superconducting qubits, by running high-fidelity mem-
ory, stability and lattice surgery experiments. We com-
bine noise-informed decoding, defect exclusion, and leak-
age post-selection to achieve significant improvements in
memory and stability, and a collection of pre- and post-
selection techniques to achieve large improvements in lat-
tice surgery. Our results reveal key lessons for future
fault-tolerant devices.
We have seen that leaky and under-performing com-
ponents can severely limit error-correction performance.
We analyze the role of leakage in our experiments by
post-selecting on runs without any detected leakage. This
leakage post-selection consistently improved performance
of logical elements. A variety of strategies exist and have
been tested for reducing leakage in error-correction ex-
periments [9–13, 91–94], and these will likely be neces-
sary in future generations of devices. Defects resulted in
bottlenecks to improving performance in our experiments
and we overcame this issue by introducing defect exclu-
sion techniques for the dynamic compass code. Large
numbers of defects will limit the code distances achiev-
able in manufactured chips, indicating that future devices
may need to improve component homogeneity in order to
see further large performance gains.
We have also seen very sizable benefits from combining
pre- and post-selection filters in lattice surgery, which is
of significant relevance to future error-correction exper-
iments. The pre-selection filters that we use in lattice
surgery experiments are qualitatively different than post-
selection techniques. Pre-selection allows us to probe the
failure mechanism associated with the logic gate itself, re-
moving those mechanisms associated with initializing in a
leaked state of the processor or failing to prepare the code
space of one or both of the codes. These filters could in
principle be applied before any long experiments are be-
gun, as part of a prepare-until-succeed loop [16]. Hence,
these could potentially be important components of fu-
ture experiments for maximizing observed fidelities. Sep-
arately, lattice surgery often features in magic state facto-
ries, which can effectively make use of post-selection [33].
Performing logical gap post-selection on lattice surgery
within such parts of a fault-tolerant architecture could
potentially be a scalable [79–82, 87] method to further
boosting resource state fidelities with the additional ad-
vantage of aborting low-confidence shots early.
Finally, we observe that good noise characterization,
accurate decoding and measurement soft information,
while improving performance up to a degree, are ulti-
mately insufficient to overcome the deleterious effects of
defects. However, we have not investigated decoders with
higher accuracy than BeliefMatching [83]. Neural net-
work decoders [95–98] or most-likely error decoders [99]
could be investigated in future work to see if these could

12
potentially overcome the need to remove certain under-
performing components. We note that the gains seen
from using soft information in stability experiments dis-
appear when using ACES-noise information. This could
be practically useful for future experiments, since collect-
ing ACES data can be costly in terms of device time and
classical processing time required. However, it would be
important to know in what regimes these findings hold.
Future generations of quantum processors will need to
improve component homogeneity, to avoid the need for
excessive defect exclusion, and will require fast, multi-
level resets or other techniques to actively remove leak-
age. They will need to maintain high component fideli-
ties and homogeneity while scaling to larger sizes of chip.
The use of defect exclusion strategies that are far less
costly in terms of distance and fidelity could be achieved
by considering designs with more connectivity between
qubits [18]. There will in general be an interplay be-
tween chip homogeneity, component fidelity, and fabrica-
tion yield, with greater qubit connectivity increasing tol-
erance to defects. We envisage that these future devices
will allow for further key error-correction primitives to be
demonstrated, such as magic state injection and distilla-
tion, a greater array of Clifford operations, and real-time
accurate decoding. Finally, future quantum processors
with a large number of logical qubits will require even
more qubits and/or other innovations such as long-range
connectivity that will allow for larger-distance quantum
codes and lower logical error rates.

B. Defect exclusion strategies
Defective components in the Boston device tend to ag-
gregate around particular locations. The couplers around
qubits 146, 46 and 85 tend to consistently underperform,
while other qubits and couplers have varying levels of
performance. We choose to limit defect exclusion to two
locations within the code patch so as to avoid reducing
the distance of the 7×7 patch to less than 5 in both bases.
On all days tested, couplers around qubits 85 and 146 had
CZgate errors that were 2-3 orders of magnitude larger
than the medianCZgate error across the device, and the
next-worst coupler was consistently neighboring qubit 46.
Meanwhile,T 1 andT 2 times were often poorest for qubits
85, 146 and 46. Specifically, on the date during which the
majority of our data was taken (2026/06/01), qubit 46
had the worstT 2 time (4.23µs) and the second-worstT 1
time behind qubit 85 (9.38µs). On another data collec-
tion date (2026/06/19), qubit 46 had the second-worst
T1 time behind qubit 85 (14.45µs) but a betterT 2 time
relative to other qubits. Owing to the underperforming
couplers and qubits in those three regions specified, we
chose to consistently treat these three locations as defec-
tive and test defect exclusion strategies on them.
We treat these defects in different ways. Qubit 146 lies
on the bottom row of the chip. We can place a 7×7 code
on the chip while avoiding the bottom row entirely and
exclude the defective component in this way. Meanwhile,
qubits 46 and 85 lie in the middle of any 7×7 code, and
must be excluded by deforming the code. Note that ex-
cluding any coupler from the code leaves one qubit with

C. Syndrome measurement circuits
Here, we provide more details on the syndrome mea-
surement circuits performed during memory and stabil-
ity experiments. We first describe the circuits used for
the regular and defect-excluded dynamic compass code,
then comment on detectors for circuits without resets,
and finally discuss details of the circuits required for a
compressed measurement schedule.
1. Dynamic Compass Code Circuits
RegularX-checks are measured using a depth-8 circuit
of CNOT gates that connect the measure qubit (blue in
Fig. 1) to the four data qubits (yellow) via theZ-measure
qubits (red) to the North and South of theX-measure
qubit. This circuit can be understood as four “via gates”,
each performing one CNOT between anX-measure qubit
and a data qubit. The via gates themselves are depth-4
CNOT circuits, although we use circuit identities to lower
the overall depth of theX-check measure circuit [19]. A
depth-2 CNOT gate circuit is then used to extract the
syndrome of a subset ofZ-checks. Measurements ofall
ZandXmeasure qubits are then made simultaneously.
The CNOT circuits for extractingXandZcheck infor-
mation are described in more detail in Ref. 19 and can
be seen in Fig. 12.
Around defects, the checks measured include three-
body and two-bodyX-checks, and single-bodyZ-checks.
These lower-weightX-checks can be measured with sub-
sets of the gates from the four-body check measurement
circuit. For two-body checks, we can also simply use a
circuit that measures out the parity information on the
Z-measure qubit in between the two data qubits. Occa-
sionally, we need to employ a different “Bell pair mea-
surement” method, outlined below.
We begin all memory experiments with a full set of
Z-check measurements followed by a full set ofX-check
measurements, which initialises the code space. In all
data plots, we count the number of rounds as the number
of measurement layers, equivalent to double the number
of full four-step syndrome extraction cycles. We start
from rounds performed after the setup of the code space.
In Fig. 12, we show the circuit used to measure the
specific checks shown in Fig. 10 and thereby implement
the memory experiment of Fig. 1. Other circuits run in
this paper are highly analogous. We show the circuit over
two rounds (four steps) of syndrome extraction.

D. Lattice surgery mechanics
There have been some different proposed implemen-
tations of lattice surgery in the literature e.g. [17, 44].
Here we set out exactly the protocol we followed (see also
Ref. 30). Unlike surface code lattice surgery, since we use
the dynamic compass code (or the standard heavy-hex
code), we can place two logical qubits in a vertical line
without requiring an additional data-qubit strip between
the two patches. This allows, for instance, two 3×3 codes
to be combined into a 3×6 code during lattice surgery
rather than a typical surface code setup which would re-
quire a 3×7 combined code. Figure 8 shows two of the
placements we use.
For the Bell fidelity experiments, we prepare each log-
ical qubit in the code space by initializing the qubits in
|0⟩and performing an initial syndrome extraction round
(which projects us into the code space). We then per-
form one more full round of syndrome extraction which
allows us to form a full set of bothX-check (phase-flip)
andZ-check (bit-flip) detectors. The codes are then com-
bined by measuring the stabilizers along the seam. After
five syndrome extraction rounds of lattice surgery we stop
measuring the checks across the seam, splitting the codes.
We perform one more full extraction round on each of the
logical qubits before measuring out the data qubits (inZ
orXbases) and inferring the appropriate logical observ-
able. If we measure out in theZbasis, the observable
is the product ¯Z1 ¯Z2 obtained on readout. If we measure
out in theXbasis, the observable is ¯X1 ¯X2 from read-
out, combined with the outcomem X of that same logical
operator obtained during lattice surgery. As described
in Section IV E, the Bell-state we have projected into is
determined by the product of the centralX-type merge-
check outcomes on the seam. The reason we perform five
rounds of syndrome extraction during the lattice surgery
is because we are operating the device without resetting
the measurement qubits. Five rounds are therefore re-
quired in order to maintain a minimum distance of 3.

F. Pre-selection filters
Here we describe the various filters applied when per-
forming pre-selection in lattice surgery experiments.
1. Initial leakage filtering
Leakage out of the computational subspace can limit
the fidelities of QEC protocols by generating errors that
are correlated over extended regions of space or across
syndrome cycles. One way to sense for leakage is to check
whether aπpulse correctly flips the state of a qubit.
To improve the sensitivity of the protocol, we had ac-
cess to an adiabatic, narrow-bandXgate, called the
Xslow gate [78]. Unlike a typicalXgate, which takes
32ns and has a spectral width of tens of MHz, theX slow
gate takes∼1µs and has a sub-MHz spectral width.
The pre-check circuit then appliesX slow-X-M pre on each
qubit. We wish to retain shots for which the measure-
mentM pre returns 0, and we wish to discard shots in any
other condition, such as the presence of leakage, which
results in measuring 1.
We note that measurement errors can also cause a
qubit to fail this check. Therefore, we apply three re-
peated measurements in place of a singleM pre. The filter
is applied if at least two of the measurements return 1.
In the Bell fidelity experiment, these pre-leakage filters
removed approximately 50% of the runs. Further details
are reported in Fig. 19.
2. Filtering on projection to the code-state
We also pre-select on successful code-state preparation.
Here we require that the initial detectors (bothZand
X) return trivial outcomes. Operationally, this removes
shots with detected faults during the initial preparation
and first syndrome-extraction rounds, before the lattice-
surgery merge. In principle such a mechanism could be
part of a prepare until succeed loop, carried out before
the rest of the circuit is executed [16]. Alternatively, this
procedure can be interpreted as an attempt to probe the
logical failures resulting from lattice surgery, rather than
poor initial state preparation.
For the teleportation protocol, three different pre-
filters were used: trivial syndrome in initial stabilizers
in the prepared basis on the source logical qubit, trivial
syndrome in initial stabilizers on the sourceandtarget
logical qubits, and trivial syndrome in the full set of ini-
tial detectors (that is, after the first two rounds of syn-
drome extraction, before lattice surgery is performed) on
both logical qubits.
Details of the yields in each experiment resulting from
these pre-filters can be found in Appendix C.

We also report results from logical state teleportation
experiments in Fig. 20. Here we use lattice surgery (im-
plementing an ¯X1 ¯X2 measurement) between two logical
qubits to teleport logical information from one logical
qubit (source) to the other (destination). For these ex-
periments we only used the 3×3 patches with allZchecks
measured every round (regular heavy-hex code).
We implement logical teleportation in the following
way:
•We prepare the source in one of the four cardi-
nal states{|0⟩L,|1⟩L,|+⟩L,|−⟩L}, and prepare the
destination in the|0⟩ L state.
•The source is then coupled to the destination only
through lattice surgery: the patches are expanded
into a joint code region and stabilizers across the
seam region are measured for five lattice surgery
rounds.
•At the end of the surgery block we perform one
further round of regular syndrome extraction on
each logical qubit (with the two code patches now
split).
•The data qubits belonging to the original source
region are destructively measured in theZbasis.
•Finally the destination logical qubit is measured in
the same basis in which the source was prepared.
Performing lattice surgery for five rounds maintains
the effective distance of the operation in the absence of
mid-circuit reset. See Fig. 20(b) for a diagram showing
the entire procedure.
A shot is counted as successful when the decoded des-
tination logical observable agrees with the logical state
initially prepared on the source. We repeat this proce-
dure independently for the four input states and report
the corresponding logical teleportation success probabil-
ities.
We now comment on the pre- and post-selection filters
and their yields for the logical teleportation protocol. In
this experiment, we found that it was less important to
pre-filter up to the first full set of detectors. Rather,
the initial stabilizer readouts (in the same basis of state
preparation) could be used for pre-filtering, with minimal
further gain in fidelity found from pre-filtering further on
a full set of stabilizers. Details of yields and the impact
on fidelity of the three different pre-selection filters used
for teleportation can be found in Fig. 20(c) (see also Sec-
tion IV F). One reason that the teleportation protocol is
less sensitive to pre-selecting out errors in the opposite
basis to Bell state preparation, is that the destination
logical qubit is only ever read out in that basis. Con-
trast this with the Bell fidelity protocol where the logical
qubits can be read out in the complimentary basis.
For logical gap post-selection in teleportation experi-
ments, PyMatching did not perform as well as correlated
PyMatching in assessing the probability of corrections
being accurate, so in this case we used correlated Py-
Matching [103] to calculate a logical gap. Shots that
were accepted were then decoded also using correlated
matching. In addition we found pre-selecting on cor-
rectly preparing the initial state was less important to
achieve high fidelity teleportation. Figure 20(a) shows
the result of applying the logical gap post-selection with-
out any other filters, and (c) shows the results of applying
the filters together with logical gap post-selection.
