An infinite-dimensional Skorokhod problem
with a diagonalization approach
LOUIS T. CLARKE∗, GUODONG PANG∗ AND RUOYU WU∗∗
Abstract.We study an infinite-dimensional Skorokhod problem on the positive cone ofL 1([0,1]),
where a regulator process constrains a free c` adl` ag path to remain nonnegative through an oblique
reflection mechanism determined by a positive linear integral operatorFonL 1([0,1]) with spectral
radius less than 1. This constrained processZremains in the positive cone, and the regulatorYis
nondecreasing with respect to the cone order and satisfies a pointwise complementarity condition,
increasing only on those space-time regions where the corresponding component ofZvanishes.
This formulation extends the classical multidimensional Skorokhod problem on the nonnegative
orthant to an infinite-dimensional setting. Under suitable assumptions on the operatorFthat
guarantee a dominant positive eigenfunction, we proceed through a diagonalization argument to
give an alternative representation of the system in which theL 1 norm of the linear operator is
less than 1. From this characterization, we establish existence and uniqueness of solutions for
arbitrary input pathsX∈D([0, T], L 1([0,1])). We further show that the associated Skorokhod
map is well defined and Lipschitz continuous with respect to both the uniform and SkorokhodJ 1
topologies. As a consequence, solutions may be obtained as limits of multidimensional Skorokhod
problems, providing a constructive characterization of the infinite-dimensional reflection mechanism.
These results provide a framework for the study of constrained stochastic systems with infinitely
many interacting components and provide a foundation for reflected stochastic processes evolving
in function spaces.
1.Introduction
The Skorokhod problem provides a mechanism for constraining a path to remain within a pre-
scribed domain through the action of a regulator process. In the classicald-dimensional setting on
the nonnegative orthant, the reflected path is represented as
z(t) =x(t) + (I−F)y(t),(1.1)
wherexis the unconstrained input path onD([0,T],R d), andyis a nondecreasing regulator chosen
so thatzremains nonnegative, andIis the identity matrix. We refer tozas the reflected process
andyas the regulator. The matrixFdetermines the directions in which the regulating force acts
at the boundary, and has spectral radius less than 1. WhenF≡0, the reflection occurs along the
coordinate directions and coincides with the standard orthogonal reflection. More generally, the
term (I−F)yallows the regulator to act in directions that are not normal to the boundary, leading
to an oblique reflection mechanism. This has been extensively studied, see [8, 3, 15, 4, 7], and has
seen particular application to Jackson networks and other stochastic network and queueing models.
In [5], this concept was recently extended to an uncountably infinite dimension, considering paths
inD([0,T],L 1([0,1])) and replacing the nonnegative orthant with the positive cone ofL 1([0,1]).

The Skorokhod problem originated in 1961 with the seminal work of Skorokhod [13], who intro-
duced diffusion processes in one dimension with reflecting boundaries and established existence and
uniqueness results. In this setting, reflection was studied primarily as a mechanism for constructing
stochastic processes. This was further studied by McKean in [10, 11], looking at multi-dimensional
diffusions and stochastic boundary problems. The multi-dimensional problem was further studied
by Tanaka in [14], studying reflected Brownian motion with normal reflection. The theory was
significantly extended in 1981 by Harrison and Reiman [8], who considered reflected Brownian
motion in the nonnegative orthant and introduced the multidimensional reflection equation (1.1).
Subsequent developments shifted attention from specific diffusion processes to the deterministic
mapping that associates an unconstrained path with its reflected counterpart. The work by Chen
and Mandelbaum [2] studied the problem inD([0,T],R d) and developed geometric interpretations
of the regulator through production-set formulations and further clarified the structure of oblique
reflection. This framework was further developed by Whitt [15, Chapter 14], formalizing the notion
of a regulator set. A more abstract perspective was introduced by Dupuis and Ishii [6], who stud-
ied the Skorokhod map inC([0,T],R d) as a deterministic operator on path spaces and established
fundamental continuity and Lipschitz properties.
When studying the finite-dimensional Skorokhod problem posed by (1.1), there are two distinct
approaches to establish existence, uniqueness, and Lipschitz continuity. In [8], the solution to the
Skorokhod problem is characterized as the unique fixed point of a mapping which is shown to
be a contraction through a diagonalization argument on the routing matrix. Here, the diagonal
matrixDtakes entries from the dominant eigenvector ofF T such thatDFD −1 has the maximum
column sum equal to its spectral radius, specified as being less than 1. This property allows for
direct contraction mapping arguments to be used in the arguments for existence, uniqueness, and
Lipschitz continuity. Through the introduction of the regulator set, works such as [2] and [15] do
not rely on a diagonalization argument. Instead, they show that the regulatory(t) is the infimum
of the set{w∈D([0,T],R d) :x+(I−F)w≥0}. Existence is proved by showing that the regulator
set is nonempty, and uniqueness follows from the definition of the infimum. This instead relies on
the properties ofF, in particular that as the spectral radius is less than 1, it must hold for some
k≤dthatF k has maximum column sum less than 1. From this, one can formk-stage contraction
arguments similar to those of the diagonalized case, from which continuity and Lipschitz properties
can be established.
In moving from finite to infinite dimension, one must formalize similar concepts moving from
processes in the spaceD([0,T],R d) toD([0,T],L 1([0,1])). In [5, Section 3], we adapt the latter of
the two methods mentioned in the paragraph above. In this case, the regulator set is extended to
this new space, namely{W∈D([0,T]L 1([0,1])) :X+ (1−F)W≥0}. Here, the solutionYto
(1.2) is given by the pointwise infimum of the regulator set, withZfollowing its definition in the
same equation. One then shows that the regulator can equivalently be written as the unique fixed

In this paper, we instead adopt the diagonalization approach first introduced in [8]. For the
finite-dimensional Skorokhod problem, to proceed with a diagonalization argument it is sufficient
to simply assume that the spectral radius of the transition matrix is less than 1. However, one must
take more care when dealing with operators, as the behavior of a general positive diagonal system
may not restrict boundedness of the constants associated with Lipschitz conditions to be finite. To
be more preceise, if diagonalization causes the sytem to either vanish or explode at certain points,
then these points will cause issues when converting back to the original system. This imposes a
new set of assumptions on the reflection operatorF, ensuring thatFis both eventually strongly
positive and quasi-compact (see Assumption 2.1). Under this framework, we establish existence
and uniqueness of solutions to the Skorokhod problem in Theorem 2.1. To do this, we introduce
a mappingπ X,F on the function space for the diagonalized system that is shown to have a unique
fixed point that satisfies the Skorokhod problem.
We next consider Lipschitz continuity of the reflection map, first in the∥·∥T,1 metric. By creating
two iterative processes using the mappingπ X,F so each converges to the fixed point characterized
by the regulator of the Skorokhod problem, we prove two results on Lipschitz continuity, first in
the free processXin Theorem 5.1, and then in the reflection operatorFin Lemma 5.2. Combining
these two results, along with a further result allowing two separate processes to share the same
diagonal operator if their reflection operators are close enough in theL 1 norm, provides a Lipschitz
continuity result in both the free process and the reflection operator in Theorem 5.3. This is
extended to Theorem 5.4 for a sequence of processes that converge to a limit process. We also
study the corresponding Lipschitz continuity properties of the refleciton map under the Skorokhod
J1 metric (see the metric defined in (5.11)). To work with this topology, we first present an explicit
result on time rescaling of the reflection map, in Proposition 5.1. This provides the necessary theory
to prove results in Lipschitz continuity with this new metric. with respect to the free process in
Theorem 5.5 and with respect to both the free process and reflection operator in Theorem 5.6.
The Skorokhod problem plays a fundamental role in the study of constrained stochastic systems.
A prominent example arises in Jackson queueing networks, where queue lengths must remain non-
negative and the cumulative idle-time process when a station is not serving any customers acts as
a regulator preventing excursions below zero. In the case when the spectral radius of the transition
matrix is less than one, this represents an open Jackson network, where jobs can enter and leave the
system at different nodes. In heavy traffic, for the queue-length and workload processes, fluid limits
are typically given as the deterministic solution of the Skorokhod problem, while diffusion approx-
imations are described by reflected Brownian motions whose sample paths are obtained through
solutions of an associated Skorokhod problem, see [15].
The infinite-dimensional Skorokhod problem can see application arising naturally in systems
with a continuum of interacting components, such as measure-valued population models, stochas-
tic transport and storage systems, and large-scale (spatial) queueing networks indexed by a con-
tinuous parameter. In these settings the state of the system is more naturally represented by
a function or measure rather than a finite-dimensional vector, leading to reflection problems on
infinite-dimensional spaces. This paper along with [5] can therefore provide a foundation for the
analysis of constrained stochastic processes evolving in function spaces and for the study of contin-
uum limits of large interacting systems with certain constraints.

Remark 5.4.Theorem 5.4 shows convergence of systems where both the free process and the re-
flection operator converge, stating that the regulator and reflected process will also converge. This
is particularly useful when dealing with convergence from a finite to an infinite-dimensional system,
where the finite-dimensional system can be represented by blockwise-constant processes and opera-
tors. This is the case in our previous work[5]. Because the continuity is Lipschitz in both the free
process and the reflection operator, if one can show that both of these converge at a particular rate,
then one can also derive rates of convergence for the regulator and reflection map.
