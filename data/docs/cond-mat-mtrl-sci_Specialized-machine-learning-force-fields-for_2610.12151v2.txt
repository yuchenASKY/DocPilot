Abstract 
Machine learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) are transforming the length and time scales 
accessible to atomistic simulation. Equivariant graph neu ral networks, combined with large 
first-principles datasets, have produced pretrained models with broad chemical coverage and 
zero-shot performance for static and near-equilibrium properties. Translating this breadth into 
reliable performance in complex dy namics, however, requires validation for the intended 
conditions and observables. This Review examines how physics-driven specialization extends 
the reach of MLIPs in complex materials dynamics. Three considerations guide this assessment. 
First, training d istributions dominated by near -equilibrium structures can leave reactive or 
highly strained configurations undersampled, while representation bias and electronic-structure 
reference errors impose distinct limitations. Second, the computational cost of high -order 
equivariant architectures and multi -hop message passing can restrict accessible system sizes 
and simulation times. Third, field -coupled and open -system applications may require 
additional state variables or nonlocal interactions; force conservativit y is a separate 
architectural choice, independent of chemical coverage. Through four overlapping application 
contexts—electrified interfaces under constant potential, open systems with fluctuating particle 
numbers, phase transformations and multiphase evol ution, and large -scale deformation and 
fracture—we connect these requirements to observable-specific validation. Foundation models 
and specialized potentials occupy complementary positions in this design space. A pretrained 
model can be used directly where it meets the target requirements; the benefits of targeted 
adaptation should be established through the intended observables and suitable task-compatible 
baselines. We conclude with a roadmap for physically consistent, hardware-aware force fields 
that connect electronic-structure references to validated atomistic mechanisms and larger-scale 
materials models. 
1. Introduction 
In modern computational materials science and chemical engineering, a fundamental 
understanding of atomic -scale microstructural evolut ion is the cornerstone for elucidating 
macroscopic properties, such as catalytic dynamics, phase transition mechanisms, and 
mechanical failure. Since the formulation of the Born -Oppenheimer approximation, the 

2 
accurate and efficient construction of the potential energy surface (PES) has remained a central 
and demanding task in molecular simulations. 
On the one hand, first-principles methods, predominantly density functional theory (DFT) and 
ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD), describe atomic interactions th rough electronic -
structure calculations; in standard Born –Oppenheimer AIMD, the electrons follow the nuclei 
adiabatically rather than undergoing explicit nonadiabatic dynamics [1, 2] . However, the 
computational complexity of st andard Kohn -Sham DFT typically scales cubically with the 
number of electrons ( 𝒪(𝑁3) ). Although linear -scaling ( 𝒪(𝑁) ) methods based on 
approximations such as localized Wannier functions have been developed, their efficiency 
depends on electronic locality, matrix decay, numerical tolerances, and the system size at which 
reduced asymptotic scaling offsets implementation overhead [3]. This stringent scaling relation 
imposes a formidable computational bottleneck, often restricting routine AIMD simulations to 
spatial domains of hundreds of atoms and temporal scales of tens of picoseconds (ps). 
Consequently, direct first -principles dynamical simulations remain computationally 
prohibitive for rare events involving high-energy barrier crossings, the prolonged relaxation of 
complex solid -liquid interfaces, and the slow kinetic processes inherent to heterogeneous 
catalysis [4]. 
On the other hand, to overcome these spatiotemporal limitations, the field has long sought to 
develop empirical interatomic potentials (EIPs), aiming to reduce computational complexity to 
linear scaling ( 𝒪(𝑁)) via analytic functional forms. This trajectory represents a progressive 
evolution from simple to complex formulations. Early model s predominantly relied on pair 
potentials, such as the Lennard -Jones potential for dispersion and short -range repulsion [5], 
and ionic pair-potential forms that combine long-range Coulomb interactions with Born–Mayer 
or Buckingham short-range repulsion [6, 7]. However, pure pair potentials generally cannot 
represent the directional bonding and environment-dependent energetics required for many real 
crystals and defects. Consequently, researchers intr oduced many -body terms incorporating 
angular and directional dependencies. This led to the development of the Stillinger-Weber (SW) 
potential [8] and the bond -order-based Tersoff potential [9] for covalent semiconductor 
systems, as well as the embedded -atom method (EAM) [10], specifically designed to 
approximate the embedding of atoms in the electron density of metals. 
To further simulate the critical processes of c hemical bond dissociation and formation within 
a classical framework, researchers extended the bond -order concept to develop reactive force 
fields, such as ReaxFF [11], and the Charge-Optimized Many-Body (COMB) potential, which 
incorporates dynamic charge equilibration and electronegativity equalization mechanisms [12, 
13]. 
Empirical interatomic potentials (EIPs), including reactive force fields, rely on chosen 
analytical forms and fitted parameter s. These choices can limit flexibility and transferability 
outside the chemical environments and thermodynamic conditions used for parameterization, 
although a well -designed empirical model can remain highly effective within its domain. 
Reactive PESs conta in many minima and saddle regions, so extending one fixed functional 
form across diverse chemistries can require substantial expert effort and validation [14]. MLIPs 

3 
offer a more flexible alternative, but they exchange explici t functional-form assumptions for 
assumptions about representation, data, optimization, and extrapolation. 
Confronted with the dual challenges of limited transferability and analytical complexity in 
physical modeling, the field has undergone a data -driven paradigm shift. Machine learning 
interatomic potentials (MLIPs), acting as surrogate models for first -principles calculations, 
replace many hand-parameterized interaction terms with flexible learned functions, while still 
requiring architectural choices such as descriptors, symmetry constraints, cutoffs, and energy 
decompositions. Universal-approximation results motivate the expressive capacity of neural 
networks and kernel methods under appropriate assumptions. Data efficiency, extrapolation 
reliability, and physical consistency depend on the representation and training procedure. MLIP 
construction is therefore a constrained regression problem: learning mappings from atomic 
configurations to energies and related derivatives while respecting the relevant symmetries and 
conservation laws [15]. 
A key step was to represent the many -atom potential energy surface through local atomic 
environments. In 2007, Behler and Parrinello introduced the landmark high-dimensional neural 
network potential (HDNNP) architecture [16]. This framework incorporated the key 
assumption of "local energy decomposition," which posits that the total energy of a system can 
be approximated as the linear sum of energy contributions from individual local atomic 
environments (𝐸 = ∑ 𝐸𝑖
𝑁
𝑖=1 (ℛ𝑖)). By mapping the topological neighborhood of each atom into 
translationally, rotationally, and permutationally invariant symmetry functions (e.g., ACSF), 
HDNNP converts PES fitting into local-environment regression problems that can be evaluated 
in parallel, subj ect to the validity of the locality approximation for the target system. 
Subsequently, the Gaussian approximation potential (GAP) proposed by Bartó k et al., 
combined with the SOAP descriptor, further established a rigorous mathematical formalism for 
this local mapping within a kernel regression framework [17]. 
Building upon this local -energy representation, MLIPs have achieved a long -sought dual 
breakthrough in the physics community. In terms of accuracy, within a sufficiently sampled 
domain, MLIPs can reproduce the PES of a chosen electronic-structure reference (e.g., DFT or, 
for suitably small training systems, CCSD(T)) with small interpolation errors. In terms of 
efficiency, they bypass self-consistent electronic-structure iterations. Local models with fixed 
cutoffs and bounded neighbor counts can achieve asymptotically linear scaling with atom 
number, whereas models with explicit long -range interactions, global attention, or charge 
equilibration may have different scaling and communication costs [18]. 
This characteristic endows MLIPs with substantial capabilities. Large -scale complex 
simulations previously precluded in AIMD due to computational constraints, such as fracture 
dynamics in materials c omprising millions of atoms, microsecond -scale solid -liquid phase 
transitions, and in situ reaction network exploration on heterogeneous catalytic surfaces, can 
now be realized on supercomputers or even single-node GPUs [14, 19]. Consequently, MLIPs 
help bridge the spatiotemporal gap between "microscopic electronic structures" and 
"mesoscopic thermodynamic statistics" in multiscale physical modeling. 
It is important to acknowledge that recent optimizations in underlying network operators and 
the accumulation of massive datasets have successfully catalyzed the development of 

4 
"universal machine learning interatomic potentials" (universal MLIPs) that span the periodic 
table (e.g., M3GNet [20], CHGNet [21], and MACE -MP-0 [22]). Supported by high -
throughput materials databases (such as the Materials Project or OQMD) [23, 24, 25] , these 
foundation models have demonstr ated useful zero-shot generalization capabilities in ground -
state (0 K) static structural relaxations, lattice elastic property predictions, and near-equilibrium 
phonon spectra calculations, thereby substantially accelerating the throughput of static 
materials discovery. 
However, when the research focus shifts from static materials property screening to complex 
chemical dynamics involving drastic configurational evolution, the reliance on static ground -
state data reveals underlying physical limitations. Realistic complex chemical evolutions, such 
as ion desolvation at battery interfaces under constant operating voltages, composition -
changing surface growth and reconstruction, or bond dissociation in heterogeneous catalysis, 
often involve abrupt changes in at omic coordination and require the system to traverse high -
energy transition states (TS) [26]. For models trained predominantly on low-energy structures 
and relaxation trajectories, this data -distribution "cold bias" can limit reliability when 
simulations visit poorly sampled reactive or highly strained configurations [27]. 
In these domains, equivariance or a larger receptive field does not by itself establish reliability 
beyond the sampled configurations. In reactive molecular dynamics (MD), extrapolation 
beyond the sampled configurations can produce an unphysical PES and un stable trajectories 
[28]. In essence, the performance ceiling of modern MLIPs in reactive systems is dictated not 
only by the expressive capacity of the network architecture but also fundamentally constrained 
by the physical s ampling fidelity of the training datasets. For reactive applications, sampling 
relevant pathways and high -energy environments can improve coverage; the required 
configurations depend on the target process [26]. 
Reliable dynami cs requires separate checks of extrapolation error and energy drift. Local -
energy models should preserve extensivity, with forces derived from the modeled energy. NVE 
drift also depends on PES smoothness, integrator, timestep, constraints, and numerical 
precision [29, 30]. Long-range electrostatics and polarization require separate treatment when 
relevant. Broader sampling cannot correct systematic electronic-structure reference errors. 
This review connects model design and sam pling to the physical requirements of materials 
dynamics. Foundation models provide broad interaction priors and can serve directly as 
potentials in validated domains [22, 31] . The focus is on how targeted sampling, physical 
variables, and efficient deployment support physics -driven specialization for demanding 
applications. Figure 1 organizes the available design choices, including symmetry -aware 
representations, targeted sampling, explicit ensemble variables, and physical val idation. The 
subsequent sections review architectural development (Section 2), targeted sampling (Section 
3), model selection and adaptation in four application contexts (Section 4), and scientific -
consistency criteria (Section 5). Together, these sections link application requirements to 
observable-specific tests and deployment constraints.

2. Representations and network architectures 
To overcome the generalization bottlenecks of empirical physical modeling, MLIPs have 
undergone a rapid revolution in their underlying mathematics and network architectures over 
the past decade. These complementary approaches span “local scalars → topological networks 
→ 3D equivariant tensors → multidimensional physical degrees of freedom.” Their relative 
accuracy and cost depend on the target system, training data, and implementation (Figure 2).

2.1. Local invariant representations 
When introducing machine learning to atomic -scale simulations, the central mathematical 
challenge is that raw Cartesian coordinates depend on the reference frame, whereas the model 
must obey the relevant physical symmetries. This is because the total energy and atomic forces 
of a system must strictly preserve invariance (or equivariance) under translations and rotations 
of the reference frame (i .e., 𝐸(3) symmetry), and the permutation of identical atoms should 
not alter the macroscopic state. Consequently, the first generation of MLIPs established a two-
step paradigm: "geometric feature engineering followed by nonlinear regression." Depending 
on the choic e of regressor (neural networks vs. kernel methods) and the local environment 
mapping strategy, this generation diverged into two complementary evolutionary branches. 

7 
The first is Atom -Centered Symmetry Functions (ACSF) and High -Dimensional Neural 
Networks. The foundational work for this branch was proposed by Behler and Parrinello in 
2007 [16]. They introduced the assumption of "local energy decomposition," which 
approximates the total energy of a macroscopic system as the sc alar sum of energy 
contributions from individual local atomic environments. To enable neural networks to 
"comprehend" these local environments, they designed ACSF. Using radial and angular 
symmetry functions, ACSFs map the local three-dimensional geometry to a fixed-length vector 
of continuous, rotationally and translationally invariant values (Figure 2a). 
HDNNPs based on finite -cutoff ACSFs have asymptotically linear inference cost in system 
size when the atomic density and number of neighbors per atom remain bounded. Their actual 
cost also depends on neighbor -list construction, species count, and implementation. These 
architectures provided important methodological foundations for later frameworks such as 
Deep Potential, which introduced learned local-environment representations. 
Bartó k et al. proposed the Gaussian Approximation Potential (GAP) in 2010 [17], subsequently 
introducing the Smooth Overlap of Atomic Positions (SOAP) descriptor [32]. SOAP expands 
a smooth neighbor density in radial and spherical-harmonic basis functions. By constructing a 
power spectrum, it naturally integrates out the rotational degrees of freedom to achieve 
rotational invariance. 
As a representative non-parametric kernel method, the GAP/SOAP framework established an 
accuracy benchmark in materials science [33, 34]. Its core advantages include data efficiency 
and a model -based uncertainty estimate. In data -scarce regimes, kernel methods can 
approximate reference electronic -structure targets using hundreds to thousands of 
configurations; for example, SOAP-based Gaussian process regression has been used to predict 
hydrogen-atom-transfer barriers in proteins [35]. Ga ussian-process posterior variances can 
support active learning, but they are calibrated uncertainty measures only to the extent that the 
kernel, noise model, and training distribution are appropriate. Jinnouchi et al. integrated 
Bayesian error indicators into VASP for on-the-fly training [36], and Caro et al. used Gaussian-
process uncertainty estimates to assess local environments encountered during amorphous -
carbon deposition [37]. These applicati ons demonstrated the robustness of GAP in several 
complex solid -state settings (e.g., defect evolution in tungsten [38], polymorphic silicon 
systems [39], and amorphous carbon networks [40]). This robustness reflects both descriptor 
expressivity and targeted sampling, providing an early paradigm for the physics -targeted 
strategies discussed in Section 3. 
Related whole -molecule kernel approaches include gradient -domain machine learning 
(GDML), which learns conservative molecular force fields from reference forces [41]. Its 
symmetry-adapted extension, sGDML, incorporates molecular permutation symmetries and 
was demonstrated using coupled-cluster reference data for selected molecules [42]. 
After ACSF and SOAP established the paradigm of local invariant features, the field faced an 
engineering trade-off: neural networks can require substantial data and careful opti mization, 
whereas full Gaussian -process training scales cubically with the number of training 
environments unless sparse approximations are used. GAP inference in molecular dynamics 
instead scales with the number of retained sparse points and local environ ments. To balance 

8 
expressive capacity and computational efficiency, force-field frameworks based on high-order 
features combined with linear regression emerged. An early representative of this branch is the 
Spectral Neighbor Analysis Potential (SNAP) proposed by Thompson et al. in 2015 [43]. The 
core concept of SNAP involves projecting local atomic densities onto a 4D hypersphere and 
expanding them via 4D hyperspherical harmonics to construct bispectrum components as 
rotationally invariant descriptors. Requiring only linear parameter fitting, SNAP demonstrated 
high execution speeds. Wood and Thompson extended SNAP with quadratic combinations of 
bispectrum components, finding that larger training sets were needed to control overfitting [44]. 
An explicit multielement formulation subsequently improved chemical resolution [45]. The 
W–Be helium-bubble application is discussed with its original study in Section 4.3. 
Moment ten sor potentials (MTPs) construct invariant basis functions by contracting local 
moment tensors [46]. D -optimal active learning uses the linear design matrix to identify 
extrapolative configurations [47]; its extrapolation grade is a model -dependent selection 
criterion. The MLIP package supports this workflow [48]. Applications include 
multicomponent-alloy searches [49], gas-phase reactions such as S + H 2 [50], and promoted 
Ag catalysts for ethylene epoxidation [51]. 
The atomic cluster expansion (ACE) [52] and related polynomial invariants [53] provide 
systematic bases for local atomic energies. Completeness results apply under specified 
regularity, cutoff, and truncation conditions [54]. Efficient recursive evaluation, as 
implemented in PACE, reduces the practical cost of higher-order bases [55]. Applications span 
organic conformations [56], NH3 dissociation [57], and Diels–Alder reactions in solution [58]. 
Their accuracy and efficiency depend on basis size and the sampled domain, making compact 
expansions useful for selected reactive tasks. 
Alongside linear and polynomial expansions such as SNAP and ACE, neural networks 
provided flexible nonlinear mappings from local descriptors to atomic energies. 
As early as the 1990s, Blank et al. attempted to directly map atomic coordinates to the PES 
using neural networks [59]. These early fits used restricted s ets of degrees of freedom, 
including CO/Ni(111) and a 12-dimensional H₂/Si(100) interaction surface, establishing neural 
networks as potential-energy surrogate models. A key development came in 2007 when Behler 
and Parrinello’s BPNN (the aforementioned HDN NP) established the modern paradigm of 
"local descriptors + element -specific neural networks" [16]. In 2011, Behler systematically 
derived the mathematical forms of various radial and angular symmetry functions [60], which 
laid the foundation for neural network force fields over the subsequent decade. BPNNs have 
found broad application at complex interfaces; for example, Romano et al. used BPNNs to 
simulate interfacial water dissociation, proton tr ansfer, and water -layer organization at the 
magnetite-liquid water interface [61]. For ionic systems, Ghasemi et al. combined neural -
network predictions of environment -dependent atomic electronegativities with global charge 
equilibration to determine the charge distribution and energy [62]. 
However, traditional BPNNs relied heavily on tedious parameter tuning tailored to specific 
material systems. To propel neural network force fields toward true " general-purpose" 
applicability, Smith et al. proposed the ANI -1 potential (ANAKIN -ME) in 2017 [63]. By 
modifying ACSF to capture richer angular information, ANI successfully achieved 

9 
generalization across vast organic molecula r spaces. Subsequently, this series achieved 
improvements in predictive accuracy and chemical coverage: Smith et al. introduced transfer 
learning, using inexpensive DFT data for pre -training to successfully fine -tune the general 
neural network (ANI-1ccx) to the highly expensive CCSD(T)/CBS coupled -cluster accuracy 
standard [64]; Devereux et al. then extended the architecture to complex systems containing 
sulfur and halogens (S, F, Cl) with ANI -2x [65]. Coupled with the efficient, open -source 
automatic differentiation platform TorchANI [66], non-GNN architectures based on refined 
descriptors provided practical tools for molecular modeling and conformational searches. A 
related molecular modeling approach, AIMNet2, combines iterative message passing, charge 
updates constrained by the total molecular charge, and explicit electrostatic and dispersion 
terms to describe neutral and charged species [67]. 
For materials dynamics, the neuroevolution potential (NEP) offers another efficient local 
neural-network formulation. NEP combines radial and angular invariant descriptors with a 
neural network trained using a separable natural evolution strategy [68]. Its GPU -oriented 
implementation in GPUMD integrates potential construction with atomistic simulation, 
including heat -transport calculations [69]. This approach complements message -passing 
architectures when local descriptors meet the required accuracy and high simulation throughput 
is a priority. 
Deep Potential (DP) introduced learned representations of local atomic environments [70]. 
DeepPot-SE provided a smooth embedding suitable for dynamical simulations [71], and 
DeePMD-kit enabled training and deployment [72]. Standard DeepPot -SE remains a finite -
cutoff local model unles s supplemented with nonlocal interactions. Its large -scale 
implementations are discussed separately in Section 4.5. 
Standard short-range forms of ACSF, SOAP, ACE, and DeepPot-SE share a finite spatial cutoff. 
This supports efficient local evaluation at bou nded density. It also limits the information 
available to describe interactions whose energetics depend on the wider environment. The 
consequences differ for chemical reactivity, long-range response, and tensorial observables. 
Long-range interactions also limit the chemical context available to a local model. In non -
uniform systems (e.g., polarized interfaces, charged defects, heterogeneous catalytic surfaces), 
Coulombic electrostatic forces and van der Waals dispersion forces decay very slowly. Strict 
local truncation gives the model limited receptive field, rendering it unable to perceive long -
range fluctuations in charge density beyond the cutoff sphere. Consequently, predictions of 
dielectric responses and macroscopic polarizability are prone to failure. 
Local descriptors do not imply fixed chemical bonds. Neighbor lists and descriptors can change 
continuously as atoms move, allowing local MLIPs to describe bond breaking and formation. 
The remaining limitation concerns information beyond the receptive fi eld and configurations 
outside the validated training domain. Message passing can broaden that field, but is not a 
prerequisite for chemical reactivity. 
Tensorial responses require an appropriate output construction. Scalar invariant features can 
contribute through symmetry -adapted readouts or derivatives with respect to external fields. 
Equivariant features provide a complementary route that represents transformation rules within 
the network. 

10 
Zuo et al. compared GAP, MTP, neural-network potentials, linear SNAP, and quadratic SNAP 
across six elemental systems, illustrating model - and dataset-dependent accuracy–cost trade-
offs [73]. 
First-generation MLIPs remain effective for many system-specific problems but often require 
deliberate sampling and parameterization. Finite -cutoff descriptors without explicit nonlocal 
variables cannot represent interactions that depend on global charge redistribution or 
polarization. Message -passing GNNs expand the effective receptive field and reduc e some 
manual feature engineering. Long -range electrostatics and extrapolation remain separate 
modeling challenges. 
2.2. Scalar message-passing networks 
The "localized information" and "fixed cutoff" bottlenecks exposed by first- and quasi-second-
generation potentials when handling complex topological reorganizations and long -range 
physical responses stem from the strong spatial locality assumption inherent in their local 
descriptors (whether hand -crafted or adaptively embedded). To extend the spatial conte xt 
available to each atomic representation, the computational materials community introduced the 
Graph Neural Network (GNN) paradigm, marking the formal entry of MLIPs into the second 
generation of "end-to-end" dynamic feature learning. 
An atomic configura tion can be represented as a graph 𝒢 = (𝒱, ℰ), with nodes 𝒱 encoding 
atomic species and edges ℰ connecting selected neighbors. Message -passing neural networks 
(MPNNs) iteratively aggregate and update these features [74]. The con nectivity is generally 
constructed from atomic positions rather than a fixed list of chemical bonds. 
Each iteration of message passing allows an atom to acquire information from a broader graph 
neighborhood. Kang et al. showed that extrapolation behavior d epends strongly on the 
mathematical form of the message -passing update [75]. Stacking 𝐿 local layers extends the 
receptive field to at most 𝐿 graph hops, with interactions beyond that range requiring additional 
treatment. 
Early GNNs predominantly relied on two -body distance scalars as edge features to naturally 
guarantee global rotational and translational invariance. SchNet, proposed by Schü tt et al. in 
2017, serves as the foundational work in this direction [76]. SchNet discarded pre -defined 
symmetry functions, utilizing continuous -filter convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to 
directly learn many-body interactions within the distance space, achieving high computational 
throughput. 
Scalar networks can be supplemented with additional physical terms and variables. PhysNet 
includes electrostatics and dispersion corrections [77, 78] and has been used for Diels –Alder 
dynamics [79]. SchNarc combines SchNet with surface hopping for excited -state 
photochemistry [80]. FieldSchNet includes external-field information and has modeled solvent 
effects on a Claisen rearrangement barrier [81]. These applications extend the modeled physics 
while retaining task-specific requirements for training and validation. 
To endow scalar networks with directional awareness, Klicpera et al. proposed DimeNet, 
shifting the message -passing target from nodes to directed edges and using spherical Bessel 
functions and spherical harmonics to encode interatomic distances and bond angles [82, 83]. 

11 
Subsequently, SphereNet [84] further incorporated higher -order geometric information, 
including torsional angles. 
M3GNet [20] and CHGNet [21] extended scalar graph models to broad materials datasets. 
CHGNet used local magnetic moments as auxiliary training i nformation to improve its 
representation of chemical environments. These models support structural relaxation and 
property screening; their dynamical validity depends on the process. The Direct Integration of 
the External Potential (DIEP) representation su pplied another physics -informed embedding, 
with applications to fracture onset in carbon nanotubes [85]. Such examples motivate 
mechanism-specific evaluation alongside general benchmarks. 
The geometric extensions in DimeNet/Sp hereNet and the broader chemical coverage of 
M3GNet/CHGNet illustrate the capabilities of scalar graph networks. Their design involves 
trade-offs in geometric resolution, representation, and evaluation cost: 
Explicit angle and dihedral tuples can increase evaluation cost with coordination number. The 
scaling depends on tuple construction, cutoff, and implementation. Dense environments may 
therefore make higher -order scalar graph models expensive, even when their geometric 
resolution improves predictions (Figure 2b). 
Scalar hidden states do not carry explicit non-scalar irreducible representations. A scalar energy 
model can still produce rotationally equivariant forces by differentiation, and those forces are 
conservative when derived from a differentiable in variant energy. Generic vector or higher -
rank tensor observables require an appropriate symmetry -aware output construction or 
equivariant features. 
These costs motivated architectures whose internal features transform equivariantly, allowing 
directional information to be propagated without explicit enumeration of every high-order tuple. 
Equivariant GNNs based on spherical tensors and group representation theory are a prominent 
solution, while scalar, Cartesian, and hybrid constructions remain viable depending on the task. 
2.3. Spherical-tensor equivariant networks 
Equivariant graph neural networks (eGNNs) provide a different inductive bias for representing 
atomic environments. Invariant descriptors can encode internal angles and anisotropic 
arrangements, and differentiating a smooth invariant energy yields equivariant forces. 
Equivariant networks additionally propagate vector and higher -order tensor features with 
prescribed rotational transformation rules. This changes how geometric information is 
represented and exchanged and can improve data efficiency, without making non-scalar hidden 
features a prerequisite for physically consistent energies and forces. 
Under rotation, energy remains invariant while forces and tensorial responses transform with 
the coordin ate frame. Spherical -tensor networks encode these rules using irreducible 
representations of 𝑆𝑂(3) , including channels with 𝐿 = 0,1,2, . .. . Their hidden features 
therefore retain transformation information that scalar channels do not carry explicitly. 
Early implementations of this mathematical framework include Tensor Field Networks (TFN) 
[86] and Cormorant [87], which demonstrated how rotationally equivariant features can be 
propagated through tenso r-product operations. Subsequently, NequIP [88] established the 

12 
effectiveness of this approach for interatomic potentials. In architectures such as NequIP, 
rotating the input produces the corresponding transformation of tensor feature channels through 
Wigner-D representations. This explicit tensor transformation structure provides a geometric 
inductive bias for intermediate features and information propagation. 
Equivariance can improve data efficiency by encoding the relationsh ip between rotated 
configurations [88]. Equivariant features also support symmetry -consistent tensorial 
observables. PaiNN propagates scalar and vector features, with readouts for properties such as 
polarizability [89]. NewtonNet uses force - and displacement -inspired latent features but 
obtains physical forces by differentiating its predicted energy [90]. This energy -based 
construction enforces conservativity for a differentiable model, while numerical energy 
conservation still depends on the integration protocol. 
However, spherical -tensor equivariant networks can incur substantial computational cost. 
Many architectures combine irreducible representations using Clebsch–Gordan tensor products 
(Figure 2c), although equivariance can also be implemented through restricted products, 
Cartesian tensors, local frames, or other symmetry-preserving operators. 
Clebsch–Gordan tensor products can contribute substantially to the ev aluation cost of 
equivariant MLIPs. Increasing angular order or channel count generally increases the number 
of coupling paths and intermediate features. Runtime bottlenecks depend on tensor -product 
structure, operator implementation, numerical precision, and hardware: some workloads are 
limited by memory movement, whereas others are limited by arithmetic or kernel -launch 
overhead. Specialized kernels, fusion, and compilation can improve GPU utilization, as 
discussed in Section 6. The relevant comparison is therefore measured accuracy and throughput 
for a specified workload. 
Architectural design can reduce these costs through higher -order information within shallow 
networks or through strictly local interactions. MACE and Allegro illustrate these 
complementary strategies. 
The first pathway uses higher-body-order messages to improve the accuracy–cost trade-off of 
equivariant architectures. M3GNet and CHGNet demonstrated broad chemical coverage with 
scalar graph representations, while equivariant message-passing models introduced alternative 
ways to encode many-body geometry. The MACE architecture, proposed by Batatia et al. [91], 
addressed this challenge. MACE merges equivariant message passing with the mathematical 
essence of atom ic cluster expansion (ACE). Tensor products of aggregated node features 
construct higher-body-order local messages, enabling competitive accuracy with two message-
passing layers in the reported benchmarks. The spatial receptive field remains determined by 
the number of layers and interaction cutoffs. This shallow many -body construction provided 
the architecture for broadly pretrained models such as MACE-MP-0 [22]. 
Strictly local architectures offer a complementary route to efficient parallel evaluation. Allegro 
constructs equivariant interactions within local environments without iterative multi-hop node 
updates [92]. Its locality supports domain decomposition and large atom counts. Chemical 
coverage remains a separate training choice, while accessible simulation time depends on 
throughput and the number of integration steps. 

13 
A complementary architectural philosophy is illustrated by SUS²-MLIP, developed by Hu et 
al. [93]. The model incorporates universal-equation-of-state (UEOS) scaling into an element -
agnostic radial function, separating chemical identity from the coordinate expansion. The 
radial-coefficient space therefore loses the explicit Z² factor associated wit h element pairs, 
although element -dependent scaling parameters remain. Nonlinear radial transformations, 
motivated by associative -memory and KAN ideas, extend the expressive capacity of this 
compact framework. In the reported multielement benchmarks, SUS²-MLIP achieves accuracy 
comparable to several neural-network potentials with two to three orders of magnitude fewer 
parameters; the alloy model has 17,689 parameters, compared with over five million for DPA-
2. These benchmarks illustrate the parameter effic iency of physical scaling priors. Parameter 
count alone does not determine inference speed, memory use, or communication cost; these 
require measurements at matched accuracy and hardware. On the broader M3GNet dataset, the 
smaller SUS²-MLIP model has highe r errors than M3GNet, revealing an accuracy –
compactness trade-off. 
These approaches demonstrate that chemical coverage and computational locality are separate 
design choices. Architecture should be selected together with the required interactions, target 
observables, and available hardware. Specialization is useful when a deliberate change 
improves validated performance for those requirements. 
2.4. Extensions of representations and physical variables 
Current developments include Cartesian tensor implementat ions, equivariant attention, 
additional electronic-state variables, and physical scaling priors (Figure 2d). These directions 
address different representational and computational requirements. 
Because spherical-tensor implementations can incur costly angul ar-momentum coupling and 
irregular memory access, several groups have explored Cartesian equivariant representations. 
HotPP is an 𝐸(𝑛)-equivariant message -passing model that propagates Cartesian tensors of 
arbitrary order; reducible Cartesian tensors prese rve equivariance but can contain trace 
redundancy [94]. TACE develops spectral and spatial tensor atomic cluster expansions in 
Cartesian space and uses irreducible Cartesian tensor decomposition to organize high -order 
components; its CG -free spatial formulation provides an alternative implementation route, 
while high-order tensor evaluation remains a computational cost [95]. 
TensorNet [96], CACE [97], CAMP [98], and TACE use Cartesian constructions to organize 
geometric information and symmetry -preserving operations. Tensorial ACE extensions also 
provide bases for covariant observables. Their accuracy and compu tational cost depend on 
tensor rank, body order, basis truncation, implementation, and task. 
Alongside these tensor representations, equivariant attention combines symmetry -preserving 
features with learned information weighting. Its spatial reach depends on which environments 
are connected: attention restricted by a cutoff remains local. 
EquiformerV2 [99] uses efficient 𝑆𝑂(2)-reduced spherical operations developed in eSCN [100] 
to support higher-degree local equivariant attention. SO3krates [101] uses Euclidean variables 
to modulate attention, while eSEN [102] employs edge -aligned 𝑆𝑂(2) operations in an 
equivariant architecture. Studies of approximately learned equivariance provide further 

14 
comparisons between symmetry constraints, data scale, and task performance [103, 104] . 
Together, these approaches explore different ways to represent and implement symmetry. 
Electronic-state dependence introduces a further representational requirement. Many MLIPs 
represent one selected adiabatic PES, often for a specified charge and spin treatment. Redox 
chemistry, magnetic transitions, and nonadiabatic processes can involve a dditional electronic 
states. Modeling them may require explicit state variables or multiple coupled PESs, according 
to the chosen electronic-structure and dynamical description. 
Electronic variables can enter as inputs, latent variables, or prediction targets. DeepH predicts 
DFT Hamiltonian matrix elements for electronic-structure calculations [105]. Spin-dependent 
symmetry functions distinguish collinear spin environments [106], while fourth -generation 
HDNNPs use global charge equilibration to represent nonlocal charge transfer [107]. Magnetic 
ML potentials address spin -dependent energies and spin –lattice coupling [108, 109] . These 
constructions represent different physical quantities; any nuclear –electronic dynamics must 
remain consistent with the selected state model. 
Architecture, configurational coverage, and reference accuracy limit different aspects of an 
MLIP. Model refinement should target the dominant limitation for the intended observable and 
computational budget. Richer representations, targeted sampling, and improved electronic -
structure references address model bias, configurational coverage, and label fidelity, 
respectively. 
The Orb -v2 and Orb -v3 family, developed by Rhodes et al., deliberately departs from the 
dominant equivariant, conservative paradigm. Orb -v3 includes both direct -force and 
conservative variants based on a non-equivariant architecture and reports near-state-of-the-art 
accuracy across a range of evaluations, including properties requiring higher-order derivatives 
of the potential energy surface. Its direct -force variants achieve substantial reductions in 
latency and memory footprint relative to alternative MLIPs in the reported benchmarks [110]. 
The authors compare how equivariance, conservativity, and graph sparsity affect accuracy, 
latency, and memory use. Furthermore, Orb -v3 has demonstrated stable simulations of the 
carbonic anhydrase II enzyme—a system of over 20,000 atoms that is well outside its training 
distribution—using orb-v3-direct-inf-omat with Langevin dynamics at 300 K [110]. For NVE 
dynamics, the authors recommend the continuous cons ervative-inf variant. A stable 
thermostatted trajectory does not establish accurate equilibrium statistics, reaction kinetics, or 
transport. Thermostatting does not automatically remove systematic bias from nonconservative 
forces [111]. These distinctions motivate selecting and validating the appropriate model variant 
for the intended observable. 
These developments couple representational capacity to computational cost. Targeted 
exploration addresses gaps in configurational co verage (Section 3), while specialization for 
demanding applications may also require additional state variables, interactions, or deployment 
strategies (Section 4). 
3. Targeted sampling for materials dynamics 
As systematically detailed in the preceding sec tions, the evolution of underlying network 
architectures—from hand -crafted descriptors to high -order spherical tensors, and toward 

15 
frontier Cartesian tensors and equivariant Transformers —has pushed the fitting capacity of 
MLIPs to high levels of physical fidelity. When a model’s expressive capacity is no longer the 
primary bottleneck, the "nature of the data distribution it fits" can become a critical determinant 
of the force field’s performance limits in complex, multi-scale chemical reactions. 
3.1. Training distributions and PES softening 
High-throughput electronic -structure datasets have accelerated MLIP development. Many 
early materials datasets emphasize stable structures and relaxation trajectories, while molecular 
datasets often emphasize equilibrium conformers. These resources provide valuable 
information, but their sampling objectives need not cover the strained, reactive, or interfacial 
environments visited in a particular simulation. 
This emphasis is changing in materials foundation -model developme nt. OMat24 includes 
extensive off-equilibrium inorganic configurations and reports reduced softening in models 
trained on its more diverse data [112]. MatterSim uses active learning to sample a wide range 
of temperatures and pressures [113]. These efforts broaden the coverage of pretrained models; 
whether additional sampling is needed remains a question for the intended process and 
observables. 
PES softening provides one example of this distribut ion dependence. Deng et al. reported 
systematic underestimation of selected vibrational and energetic properties by M3GNet, 
CHGNet, and MACE -MP-0 [114]. Phonon softening indicates errors in local PES curvature 
and does not uniquely identify missing high-energy transition-state data as their cause. Surface-
energy benchmarks also identified substantial errors for selected elemental surfaces when 
broadly pretrained MLIPs were applied without task -specific adaptation [115]. Reference 
quality is a separate constraint: inaccurate electronic -structure labels cannot be corrected by 
fitting them more closely. 
Targeted additions can close coverage gaps in physically constrained models. In SUS²-MLIP, 
adding 45 FeCo3Sb4Ti4 configurations substantially improved phonon predictions for a 
previously undersampled Co–Fe interaction [93]. The example shows how focused sampling 
can complement a physical scaling prior. 
For reactive applica tions, targeted exploration of relevant transition regions and competing 
pathways can complement broad pretraining and architectural priors. The value of additional 
data should be tested through barrier estimates, forces along the sampled pathways, and the 
stability and accuracy of the intended dynamics. 
Unbiased MD often revisits accessible basins while rarely crossing high free -energy barriers 
on available timescales. Heating can broaden exploration, but it need not reproduce the 
pathways or environments relevant at operating conditions. Descriptor -based metadynamics 
can access configurations missed by conventional MD (Figure 3b) [116]. Comparisons with 
saddle-search methods likewise show that targeted exploration can provide complementary 
training structures [117]. 
Targeted sampling should complement equilibrium data rather than replace it. A model used 
for reactions or deformation must describe both the transition regions and the basins that 

16 
determine populations and relaxation. The following methods provide different ways to obtain 
that coverage.

3.2. Targeted sampling and active learning 
Active learning prioritizes configurations for reference labeling, but it does not uniquely 
determine how those configurations are generated. An exploration trajectory may remain 
trapped in one basin even when its uncertainty is small. Conversely, large model disagreement 
can arise in physically irrelevant regions. Combining uncertainty indicators with physically 
motivated exploration helps focus labeling on the intended problem. Reaction endpoints, local 
curvature, collective variables, and chemical rules provide complementary information for 
exploration. Their usefulness depends on what is known about the process and which states 
remain undersampled. 
3.2.1. Paths between known endpoints 
When the initial and final structures of a reaction are known, the Nudged Elastic Band (NEB) 
method is a widely used approach for locating a minimum-energy path. The NEB and climbing-
image NEB (CI-NEB) algorithms [118, 119] construct a chain of images connected by springs 
between reactants and products. The nudging projection removes the perpendicular spring 
component and the parallel component of the true force; the climbing image add itionally 

17 
ascends along the path to approach a first-order saddle point. Convergence and pathway quality 
still depend on the initial path, number of images, and underlying potential-energy surface. 
Pathway datasets constrain configurations between stable r eactants and products. OC20NEB 
provides catalytic reaction paths [120], while Transition1x samples organic reaction pathways 
[121]. NeuralNEB uses learned potentials to accelerate path optimizat ion [122]. 
Reinforcement-learning approaches also explore transition states and minimum -energy paths 
[123]. These methods accelerate pathway searches, with candidate paths assessed against the 
chosen electronic-structure reference. 
3.2.2. Curvature-guided and endpoint-free exploration 
When products are unknown, endpoint -based path optimization cannot define the search by 
itself. Local curvature and stochastic exploration can instead identify neig hboring basins or 
candidate saddle points. The resulting configurations complement trajectories confined to a 
known reaction channel. 
Normal-mode sampling generates displaced structures from harmonic vibrational modes near 
a stationary point. It contribute d to ANI development [63], and Hessian -informed data can 
improve curvature-related predictions [124]. Related strategies appear in ANI-1x [125], ANI-
2x [65], QM7-X [126], and organometallic reaction workflows [127]. This efficiently broadens 
local sampling, although strongly anharmonic regions and new reaction channels may req uire 
complementary exploration. 
The dimer method searches for a nearby first -order saddle using forces to approximate the 
lowest-curvature direction [128]. Waters and Rondinelli compared such structural -evolution 
strategies for constructing MLIP training sets in Zr –O systems [117]. Their results support 
combining methods that sample different regions of configuration space. Activation-relaxation 
techniques, including r-ARTn and d-ARTn, provide related endpoint-free searches [129]. 
Stochastic surface walking (SSW) combines biased displacement and local relaxation to 
explore basins and pathways [130]. Coupling SSW with learned potentials has supported global 
searches of catalytic reaction networks, including ethene epoxidation [131]. LASP integrates 
these components in an automated workflow [132]. Sampling remains dependent on t he 
explored composition, boundary conditions, and quality of the underlying potential. 
3.2.3. Biased dynamics and collective variables 
Biased dynamics uses collective variables (CVs), such as bond distances or coordination 
numbers, to promote otherwise inf requent events. The chosen variables determine which 
pathways are accelerated. Because the bias changes the sampled dynamics, extracting 
equilibrium probabilities or kinetic rates requires the corresponding statistical treatment. 
Constraints and biasing fo rces serve different roles in molecular dynamics. SHAKE enforces 
holonomic constraints during integration [133]. Restrained or steered simulations instead apply 
forces that move selected coordinates. Zhang et al. generated ANI-1xBB using bond-breaking-
oriented sampling [134], while Hong et al. used a sum of bond distances as a collective variable 
in steered MD for HF dissociative adsorption on silicon nitride [135]. 

18 
More advanced methods include umbrella sampling and metadynamics. Umbrella sampling 
[136] applies biased windows along selected collective variables and can reconstruct a free -
energy profile after appropriate reweighting; it has been combined with MLIPs for atomic -
layer-deposition and catalytic applications [137, 138]. Metadynamics [139] deposits a history-
dependent bias in collective -variable space to accelerate e scape from visited basins. Free 
energies can be recovered under method -specific convergence and reweighting conditions, 
whereas unbiased kinetic rates generally require additional treatment. Descriptor -space 
metadynamics has been used to seek undersampled atomic environments [116]; CREST uses 
metadynamics-like biases for conformer exploration [140]. ArcaNN combines concurrent 
learning with OPES for reactive pathways [141], and restricted multicanonical sampling has 
been used to broaden energy-space exploration [142]. 
3.2.4. Chemical rules and reaction-network enumeration 
Rule-based enumeration offers another route when direct trajectori es would explore too few 
reaction channels. Proposed bond rearrangements generate candidate reactions that can 
subsequently be screened and refined using electronic -structure calculations. The imposed 
rules also define which chemistry may be missed. 
YARP a utomates reaction proposals and transition -state searches using chemical 
transformation rules [143]. The associated RGD1 dataset provides an extensive collection of 
elementary organic reactions [144]. These resources can expand reactive training coverage, but 
enumeration is bounded by the allowed transformations and search settings. Applicability to 
metals or extended solids requires different structural and chemical assumptions. 
3.2.5. Concurrent learning and defect-focused sampling 
Concurrent learning links exploration, configuration selection, reference labeling, and model 
updates. Physical conditions guide exploration, while model-dependent indicators help allocate 
labeling effort. The challenge is to cover relevant events without repeatedly sampling similar 
environments. 
DP-GEN (Deep Potential Generator) established a widely used concurrent -learning workflow 
for Deep Potential models [145]. During exploration und er selected temperatures, pressures, 
and other boundary conditions, an ensemble of independently trained models provides a model-
deviation score that is used to select configurations for additional quantum-mechanical labeling. 
Ensemble disagreement serves as an extrapolation indicator whose sensitivity depends on 
model diversity and configurational coverage. Agreement can persist outside the training 
domain. Zhu et al. estimated uncertainty with a single neural network, reducing the overhead 
of ensemble-based uncertainty evaluation [146]. Kulichenko et al. used an uncertainty-derived 
bias to drive exploration toward underrepresented configurations [147]. Dataset -selection 
methods seek compact informative subsets [148]. Related workflows couple reaction rules with 
active learning (e.g., RAL) [149], use nanoreactors for exploratory sampling [150], or combine 
enhanced sampling with active learning at catalytic interfaces (e.g., DEAL) [151]. 
Extended metallic defects introduce an additional sampling problem: their characteristic 
dimensions can exceed a tractable DFT cell. Shuang et al. sampled defect environments in large 
empirical-potential simulations and reconstructed selected clusters as periodic reference cells 

19 
[152]. MLIPs trained with this approach we re tested in tungsten nanoindentation, tensile 
deformation, and fracture. The example shows how physical sampling and reference -cell 
construction can make dislocation networks and grain -boundary environments accessible to 
first-principles labeling. 
For hematite–water interfaces, Schienbein et al. used active learning to obtain a potential for 
nanosecond-scale simulations [153]. Combustion studies likewise require coverage of multiple 
intermediates and reaction channels [154, 155] . Yoo et al. used ReaxFF trajectories as 
exploration seeds and relabeled selected configurations with quantum calculations [156]. These 
workflows connect exploratory reactive trajectories to quantum-mechanical reference labels. 
Architecture and sampling constrain different aspects of a learned potential. Path searches, 
normal-mode displacements, biased trajectories, reaction rules, and defect reconstruction 
provide complementary configurations . Their combination should be chosen for the 
mechanisms and observables of interest, which motivates the application-dependent strategies 
considered next. 
4. Application requirements and physics-driven specialization 
Through the preceding discussion of arc hitecture and sampling, a central distinction emerges 
between broad chemical coverage and validated fidelity under specified conditions. Universal 
models provide useful zero -shot baselines, but their reliability does not automatically extend 
to new ensembles, external fields, reactive pathways, or length and time scales. This section 
examines four scenarios in which specialized data, physical variables, or simulation algorithms 
are often required. 
Here, specialization denotes a deliberate adjustment of model inputs, representation, reference 
data, training, or deployment for a specified system, physical condition, or observable. Its 
benefit should be assessed through the intended observables and against a suitable baseline, 
including the unadapted pretrained model where task-compatible. General choices made when 
building a broadly applicable foundation model do not, on their own, constitute downstream 
specialization. MACE-MP-0, for example, reports direct applications across diverse systems 
as well as routes to further fine-tuning [22]. 
4.1. Foundation models and routes to adaptation 
With the expansion of large -scale first-principles databases (e.g., Materials Project, OQMD), 
machine learning potentials are undergoing a paradigm s hift toward "Foundation Models." 
Universal models represented by M3GNet, CHGNet, and MACE -MP-0, trained largely on 
structures from ground -state relaxation datasets, have demonstrated useful zero -shot 
generalization potential, significantly enhancing the screening throughput for near-equilibrium 
properties (e.g., lattice constants, elastic tensors, formation energies, and phonon spectra). 
Multi-domain pretraining has expanded this approach. JMP uses a GemNet -OC directional 
message-passing backbone for joint pretraining on molecular and catalyst datasets, followed 
by downstream fine -tuning, including materials tasks [157]. Its pretraining force head is 
equivariant, whereas its backbone uses scalar node and edge features rather th an non-scalar 
spherical-tensor features. In the Deep Potential Attention (DPA) series, DPA-1/2 use attention-

20 
based descriptors [158, 159], whereas DPA-3 operates on invariant features within a line graph 
series [160]. 
Foundation models provide useful zero-shot baselines across broad chemical spaces, especially 
for properties close to the distributions represented in their training data. Reliability in reactive, 
high-energy, charged, or externally driven states depends on how these environments are 
represented in the training data and model inputs. 
DPA-3 illustrates how representation and training can be designed for broad transferability 
[160]. Its line graph series (LiGS) framework propagates invariant node and edge features and 
increases model capacity through layer stacking. Dataset -aware multi-task training supports 
heterogeneous reference data within a shared fitting framework. The DPA -3.1-3M model 
trained on OpenLAM-v1 demonstrated zero-shot force-field prediction across 12 downstream 
tasks. These benchmarks assess energy, force, or related prediction errors on the evaluated 
datasets; validation of a particular reaction trajectory or kinetic observable remains a separate 
assessment. 
To improve fidelity relative to higher -level electronic -structure references, universal 
foundation models and their derivative frameworks have extensively incorporated multi -
fidelity and delta learning (𝛥-learning). In 𝛥-learning, models explicitly fit differences between 
a lower-cost baseline and a higher-level reference, reducing the number of expensive reference 
calculations needed [161, 162]. Multi-fidelity models can instead share representations wh ile 
conditioning predictions on reference level; SevenNet-MF uses fidelity-dependent parameters 
to learn from abundant lower -fidelity and sparse higher -fidelity data [163]. MACE-MH uses 
multi-head replay post -training across electronic-structure theories and chemical domains, 
preserving information from earlier tasks while incorporating new ones [164]. These tasks need 
not form a single low -to-high accuracy hierarchy. To accommodate heterogeneous training 
tasks, UMA uses a Mixture of Linear Experts (MoLE), with routing conditioned on 
composition and global context, including charge, spin, and the targe t DFT task [165]. The 
routing distinguishes predictions associated with different reference tasks. Multitask and multi-
fidelity strategies can improve data efficiency by combining information across reference 
levels [166]. DPA -3 conditions a shared fitting framework on one -hot dataset -identity 
encodings to accommodate heterogeneous electronic -structure settings [160]. Knowledge 
distillation uses teacher -model predic tions as training targets for a student potential, as 
demonstrated for organic molecular crystals [167]. 
Multi-fidelity learning can improve accuracy relative to a chosen high -level reference while 
reducing the number of expensive labels [168]. The attainable fidelity and transferability 
nevertheless depend on the high -level con figurations used for correction. Methods such as 
canonical CCSD(T) have steep formal scaling (commonly quoted as 𝒪(𝑁7)) and are usually 
limited to comparatively small systems, so reactive and high-energy configurations may remain 
sparsely labeled. Includin g reactive configurations in the correction set extends the scope of 
the higher-level description. 
The MACE-OFF family targets organic molecular modeling: trained on the SPICE dataset and 
extended in the MACE -OFF24-SC model with softcore interactions and m essage scaling to 
enable alchemical free energy calculations [169], MACE-OFF achieves subchemical accuracy 

21 
relative to experiment for solvation free energies and demonstrates robust behavior in 
challenging organic crystal str ucture prediction tasks. These results support the use of 
chemical-domain models for the tested molecular and free -energy applications [169, 170] . 
Chemical-domain pretraining and the downstream softcore modifications are dist inct design 
choices. Meta FAIR's Open Molecules 2025 (OMol25) dataset broadens molecular reference 
coverage. OMol25 comprises over 100 million DFT calculations at the ωB97M -V/def2-
TZVPD level, explicitly including “reactive structures” and “variable charge /spin” alongside 
conformers and solvated systems [171]. A recent preprint reports 96% transition -state search 
success rates for OMol25 -trained models (MACE -OMol25, UMA-M) on the Poly25 closed -
shell polymerization benchmark us ing the native freezing -string workflow followed by DFT 
transition-state optimization [172]. The reported search result demonstrates the value of 
reactive reference data within that workflow. 
For reactive MD, validation shoul d include the bond rearrangements, short -range encounters, 
and competing intermediates visited under the target conditions. 
Short atomistic trajectories resolve local rearrangements and early -stage structural evolution. 
Simulations of lithium –electrolyte i nterfaces, for example, have proposed nanoscopic 
precursors to dendrite initiation [173]. Connecting these precursors to macroscopic growth 
requires longer-time kinetic modeling. 
Reference accuracy and configurational coverag e address different limitations. Higher -level 
labels improve the target PES only where the reference method is appropriate and the relevant 
configurations are represented. Neither additional labels nor longer trajectories alone establish 
reliable phase stability or reaction kinetics. 
Two common routes to specialization are fine-tuning a pretrained model and training a system-
specific potential. The choice depends on data availability, reference compatibility, physical 
variables, and the accuracy–cost target. 
Fine-tuning can reuse a broad representation for complex mixtures or multicomponent alloys. 
Its value depends on compatibility between the pretrained reference and the new labels, as well 
as coverage of the target configurations. For a narrower domain wi th stringent throughput 
requirements, a compact model trained from scratch can be competitive. It forfeits pretrained 
information but allows direct control of the representation and dataset. Matched comparisons 
should evaluate accuracy, dynamical observabl es, retention of required behavior, and 
evaluation cost. 
Architectural priors provide a complementary choice within either fine-tuning or training from 
scratch. The physical scaling example in Section 2.3 illustrates how representation, data 
coverage, and parameter efficiency can be considered together. 
Retaining useful pretrained behavior is an additional consideration during fine -tuning. 
Lifelong-learning and replay strategies can reduce forgetting [174], while transferable models 
can support related applications across chemical space [175]. Forgetting -aware fine -tuning 
methods, including reEWC, explicitly balance adaptation and retention [176]. Their success 
should be assessed on both the new target and the regions that the adapted potential must 
continue to represent. 

22 
The following scenarios organize these choices by physical requirement. They illustrate where 
specialization has been useful and which tests are needed to assess its value. Four settings are 
considered: potential-controlled interfaces, particle-exchanging systems, multiphase evolution, 
and large -scale deformation. These settings overlap and include equilibrium and driven 
processes. Constant-potential or reservoir sampling need not be nonequilibrium, and quasistatic 
fracture calculations do not provide an MD time axis. Each combines distinct demands on the 
potential and the simulation method. Metallic applications are included throughout because 
phase competition, evolving defects, and chemical order provide stringent tests of dynamical 
reliability. 
At electrochemical interfaces, the model must represent the response to potential, charge, and 
external fields. For open systems, the potential must support changing composition and the 
configurations generated by deposition, insertion, removal, or irradiation. For multiphase 
evolution, competing free energies, interface structures, and activated processes must be 
represented at the relevant conditions. For de formation and fracture, defect energetics must 
remain reliable at the length and time scales needed to resolve collective mechanisms. Figure 
4 gives representative examples of these complementary requirements. 
4.2. Electrochemical interfaces and constant-potential simulations 
Many foundation MLIPs are energy models conditioned on atomic species and geometry at a 
fixed electronic reference state. Unless charge, electrode potential, or an external field is 
supplied explicitly, such models cannot distinguish c onfigurations that share the same atomic 
geometry but correspond to different electrochemical boundary conditions. 
Electrochemical experiments are often controlled at an electrode potential. In an ideal constant-
potential description, the electrode can exchange electrons with a reservoir so that the 
electronic chemical potential is fixed.

constant-potential description with equilibrated electrons, the relevant qu antity is 𝛺 = 𝐸 −

consistent reference. Atomic forces follow the negative derivative of Ω with respect to nuclear 
coordinates at fixed μₑ, rather than that of the canonical energy E alone [177]. At finite 
electronic temperature, the corresponding electronic free energy replaces E in this 
transformation.

and examined atomistic interphase formation [185]. Fu et al. developed a reactive MLIP for 
Li–Si anodes to study heterogeneous in terdiffusion [186]. Both applications connect 
representative reactive configurations to a defined interfacial process. 
Electrocatalytic interfaces combine field response, solvation, structural fluctuations, and 
activated chem ical events. Uncertainty -guided labeling helps select configurations, while 
enhanced sampling can promote events that unbiased trajectories rarely visit. Their 
combination is useful when both configurational coverage and labeling cost limit model 
development. 
For oxygen reduction at an Au–water interface, Yang et al. combined neural-network potentials 
with metadynamics to sample reactive configurations [187]. Raman et al. used iterative 
learning to investigate acid –base chemis try at an IrO 2 interface [188]. Nakanishi et al. 
examined proton transfer at a Zr 7O8N4/water interface with a model adapted to its surface 
environments [189]. These examples link model construction to specific interfacial processes. 
Electrochemical simulations require a consistent description of potential, charge, field, and 
sampling conditions. Task-specific training and broadly pretrained conditional models can both 
address these requirements. Their suitability should be judged by potential -dependent 
structures, energetics, and response functions. 
4.3. Open systems and changing composition 
Open-system simulations introduce a different requirement: exchange of atoms with an 
environment. The potential must evaluate the resulting compositions and local structures, while 
the sampling algorithm determines how exchange occurs. 
Reliable open-system energies require consistent references across compositions and coverage 
of the local environments generated by particle exchange. Open-system processes such as CVD, 
ALD, plasma etching, and particle implantation involve particle exchange or imposed reactive 
fluxes and can generate short-range collisions, dangling bonds, interstitial defects, and distorted 
coordinations. Models not trained or regularized for these configurations may produce 
unphysical energies or forces and destabilize a trajectory. 

25 
Useful workflows combine variable -composition potentials with reservoir sampling, explicit 
deposition, or reactive dynamics. Training and short -range regularization must cover the 
environments generated by the chosen protocol. 
Grand-canonical sampling describes equilibrium with a reservoir at a specified chemical 
potential (𝜇). It is useful for composition -dependent surface structures and oxidation states. 
Driven growth or reactive fluxes may instead require explicitly non-equilibrium protocols. 
Conventional fixed-particle MD can sample NVE, NVT, or NPT conditions but does not by 
itself exchange atoms with a reservoir. Xu et al. coupled an Embedded Atom Neural Network 
(EANN) with grand -canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) to sample variable composition at a 
specified chemical potential [179]. 
GCMC evaluates energy differences between states with 𝑁 and 𝑁 ± 1 particles, with 
acceptance probabilities chosen for the intended reservoir conditions. The potential must give 
comparable energies across those states. Using EANN energies, Xu et al. examined oxidation-
induced coarsening and step reconstruction of Pt surfaces (Figure 4b) [179]. This coupling 
samples equilibrium structures across particle numbers, expanding the accessible composition 
space. 
Deposition and implantation generate energetic collisions and unusual local environments. 
Caro et al. simulated amorphous-carbon deposition using an existing GAP and used Gaussian-
process uncertainty estimates to assess the representativeness of local environments 
encountered during atomic impacts [37]. Cusentino et al. used a linear SNAP potential to 
investigate helium implantation and suppressed bubble nucleation in WBe 2 [190]. These 
examples link model development to process-specific configurations and defect outcomes. 
Chemical etching and atomic layer deposition (ALD) involve bond rearrangements that can be 
rare on direct MD timescales. For HF reactions with amorphous Si3N4, Hong et al. used bond-
related CVs and enhanced sampling to explore dissociative adsorption and network disruption 
[135]. Nakata et al. combined learned potentials with umbrella sampling for precursor reactions 
on silicon surfaces [137]. These approaches target specific reaction coordinate s. Free-energy 
reconstruction and kinetic interpretation require appropriate treatment of the applied bias. 
Open-system reliability depends on both the potential and the exchange protocol. Reservoir 
sampling, explicit impacts, and biased reactive trajector ies generate different configuration 
distributions. Validation should therefore include the short -range interactions, composition 
changes, and reaction channels relevant to the selected workflow. 
4.4. Phase transformations and multiphase evolution 
Phase tr ansformations and multiphase evolution place simultaneous demands on 
thermodynamic accuracy and sampling. Competing phases can differ by small free energies, 
while nucleation, diffusion, and interface migration may occur on very different timescales. 
Multiphase sintering, metallic phase transitions, and catalyst aging can involve coarsening, 
interdiffusion, phase-boundary migration, and dissolution over timescales far beyond direct 
atomistic MD. The practical time accessible to an MLIP -driven trajectory depends on system 
size, hardware, and model cost; even when nanoseconds or longer are feasible, hour- or month-

26 
scale rare events generally require accelerated dynamics or coarse -grained kinetic models. 
Phase boundaries can also be sensitive to small free -energy differences, so a model trained to 
a particular DFT approximation inherits its systematic reference error unless corrected to a 
higher-level target. 
Accelerated sampling and higher -level corrections address different parts of this problem. 
Kinetic models can extend accessible times, while multifidelity or 𝛥-learning approaches can 
refine the reference energetics. 
To extend kinetic sampling, Jung et al. coupled a trained MLIP to off -lattice kinetic Monte 
Carlo to study Pt 3Co nanoparticle degradation [180]. The potential supplied energetics for 
activated events, enabling predictions over times beyond direct MD (Figure 4c). Such 
simulations can connect vacancy processes to nanoparticle dissolution, but their temporal 
interpretation depends on the event catalog, barrier estimates, and rate assumptions. 
Sumaria et al. combined a learned potential with basin-hopping Monte Carlo to search catalyst 
structures under reactive gas conditions [191]. Basin hopping explores low -energy 
configurations by alternating perturbation and minimization. It can identify structural 
candidates missed by a short trajectory, but its search sequence does not specify a time-resolved 
reconstruction mechanism. 
Metallic transformations couple lattice rearrangements to composition and chemical order. In 
multicomponent alloys, these dependencies demand validation across both structural and 
chemical environments. Kostiuchenko et al. combined an on -lattice low-rank potential (LRP) 
with Monte Carlo simulations to examine the influence of local lattice relaxation on phase 
transitions in bcc NbMoTaW [192]. Local relaxation effects were incorporated implicitly by 
fitting energies of DFT -relaxed configuratio ns. The resulting phase behavior depends on 
composition, temperature, and chemical arrangement. 
Accurate sampling alone is insufficient if the reference misrepresents phase competition. Solid-
solid transition temperatures may depend on small free-energy differences and can be sensitive 
to the electronic-structure reference, sampling, and nuclear quantum effects. Models trained to 
GGA-level data can therefore inherit material-dependent transition-temperature errors. 
Liu et al. used a 𝛥-learning strategy for the high-temperature phase transition of zirconia [193]. 
A lower -level DFT model was used for broad sampling, and selected configurations were 
labeled with the random-phase approximation (RPA); the learned energy correction improved 
agreement with the experimental transition behavior at substantially lower cost than labeling 
the full dataset at the RPA level. 
Phase-evolution studies therefore need to distinguish pathway discovery, equilibrium sampling, 
and kinetic prediction. Direct MD, accelerated methods, and higher -level energy corrections 
serve different purposes. Their combination is useful when each component is validated for the 
transformation being modeled. 
4.5. Deformation, defect nucleation, and fracture across scales 
Plastic deformation and failure are collective processes. Their simulation requires both 
sufficient system size and reliable energetics for the defects that carry strain or initiate damage. 

27 
Recent metallic applications show how improvements in sampling a nd model efficiency can 
support mechanistic analysis. Figure 5 summarizes representative mechanisms and the distinct 
simulation protocols used to resolve them. 
Dislocation motion, grain-boundary migration, and fracture may involve large defect networks 
and repeated local events. Computational cost limits the length and duration of a trajectory, 
while training coverage limits its physical reliability. Increasing the simulated volume is 
therefore most useful when competing deformation pathways remain accurately resolved. 
Li et al. combined entropy –symmetry-guided polymorph generation with knowledge 
distillation to construct efficient potentials for metal plasticity [194]. MD of iridium nanopillars 
linked compression to stacking -fault formation and twin propagation, whereas aluminum 
showed transient twinning, detwinning, and subsequent dislocation slip. The contrasting 
pathways connect the sampled polymorphic landscape to competing deformation mechanisms. 
Distillation reduced evaluation cost while retaining the teacher model’s relevant information. 
Defect nucleation can require validation of free-energy barriers rather than force errors alone. 
Allera et al. combined dislocation-trained MLIPs for BCC Fe and W with constrained MD and 
projected-average-force integration to obtain activation free energies for kink -pair nucleation 
[195]. The resulting activation entropy varied weakly with stress and temperature over the 
investigated regimes, unlike the stron g variations obtained with some empirical potentials. 
Direct MD provided an additional check through dislocation velocities, while a thermally 
activated model reproduced experimental yield-stress trends. This connects learned dislocation 
energetics to activation thermodynamics and the resulting plastic response. 
Hydrogen can instead couple defect evolution to embrittlement. Bao et al. developed a 
neuroevolution potential for W –H and used MD to study hydrogen accumulation near voids 
and the resulting tensile response [196]. Hydrogen-rich planar structures were associated with 
suppressed dislocation activity and enhanced cracking on {100} planes under the tested 
conditions. The mechanistic interpretation links local hydrogen organization to a change in the 
competition between plastic accommodation and cleavage. 
A complementary, quasi -static view comes from Wang et al., who used MLIPs to compare 
crack extension in Nb, NbMoTaW, and Nb45Ta25Ti15Hf15 [197]. Atomic-scale resistance 
curves revealed changes in fracture mode as cracks advanced, while local chemical fluctuations 
affected cleavage, twin nucleation, and dislocation emission. The more ductile Nb –Ta–Ti–Hf 
alloy exhibited greater crack -tip blunting and resistance to propagation. Incremental loading 
and energy minimization resolve the competition between these crack -tip mechanisms in the 
quasi-static limit.

regions and predict incorrect forces or phase stability. Time-step choice, integrator, thermostat, 
precision, and model extrapolation should therefore be tested independently.

5. Physical consistency and validation 
Throughout the preceding four sections, we have seen how architectural evolution and rigorous 
sampling have propelled machine learning interatomic potentials (MLIPs) to the forefront of 
complex dynamics. Their physical consistency depends on the constraints built into the 
representation, the reference data, and the deploymen t. The following criteria apply to both 
broadly pretrained and system-specific models. 
Low test errors and stable short trajectories are useful checks, but they are insufficient on their 
own. Validation should connect architectural properties, numerical behavior, and the 
observables supporting the scientific claim. Three recurring issues are size consistency, 
nonlocal interactions, and the relationship between energies and derivatives. 
5.1. Size consistency and physical completeness 
Size consistency requires that the energy of two noninteracting subsystems equals the sum of 
their separate energies. For a periodically repeated system evaluated with consistent boundary 
conditions, an extensive short-range energy model should scale proportionally with the number 
of repeated cells; finite-size effects and long-range electrostatics can complicate comparisons 
between differently sized cells. 
For local MPNNs and equivariant potentials of the form 𝐸 = ∑ 𝐸𝑖𝑖 , additivity is built into the 
architecture: two nonintera cting replicas with identical local environments have exactly 
additive predicted energies, apart from numerical and neighbor -list effects. This architectural 
size consistency does not imply physical completeness. If charge transfer, polarization, or 
electrostatics depends on the global cell, a finite -cutoff model can predict an incorrect 
thermodynamic-limit energy even while remaining mathematically extensive. 
The relevant distinction is therefore between architectural extensivity and correct treatment of 
nonlocal physics. Charge -equilibration, Ewald-type, reciprocal-space, or self -consistent-field 
components can be necessary when observables depend on global charge redistribution. They 
supplement rather than create the basic size consistency of a local ener gy sum, and their need 
should be established for the target system and observable. 
5.2. Incorporating long-range interactions 
The success of local MLIPs is related to Walter Kohn’s nearsightedness principle for electronic 
matter. SOAP descriptors and many MACE implementations use a finite local cutoff, often of 
a few å ngströ ms, although the effective receptive field and chosen cutoff are model - and 
application-dependent. 
For ionic liquids, polar surfaces, ferroelectrics, and biomolecules, long-range electrostatics and 
dispersion may materially affect the target observables. Coulomb interactions usually require 
a controlled long-range treatment under periodic boundary conditions; dispersion decays faster 
and can sometimes be truncated with switching or tail corrections. A strictly local graph cannot 
represent a global polarization field that is not encoded in its inputs, which matters for dielectric 
response and non-analytic phonon effects such as LO –TO splitting. Methods that incorporate 
nonlocal information include LODE-type features [203], Ewald message passing [204, 205], 
reciprocal-space networks [206], charge equilibration [207], and Laplacian -eigenbasis 

31 
electrostatics [208]. Multilayer ACE [209] instead extends semilocal or intermediate -range 
interactions; expanding a finite receptive field is distinct from specifying global electrostatics 
and its boundary conditions. NequIP -LR combines equivariant local features with global 
charge redistribution [210]. These methods trade additional assumptions and computational 
cost for a broader physic al receptive field. Their formulation and boundary conditions 
determine which nonlocal interactions are represented. 
Physical scaling priors can constrain local representations, as discussed in Section 2.3. Such 
constraints complement, but do not replace, a treatment of nonlocal interactions when these 
affect the target observable.

For a time-independent Hamiltonian description in the microcanonical ensemble (NVE), forces 
should derive from the modeled energy. For a continuously differentiable force field, symmetry 
of its Jacobian,

6. Outlook 
Future progress wil l depend on matching representational capacity, physical state variables, 
reference quality, sampling, and computational cost to the intended application. The most 
useful advances will connect these choices to measurable improvements in validated 
observables and accessible simulation scales. 

32 
Geometry and atomic species do not uniquely specify every state relevant to materials 
dynamics. Charge, spin, electrode potential, or external fields may be needed when distinct 
electronic conditions share similar struc tures. Some current models already include these 
variables. Future work should establish when such extensions are necessary and how to 
maintain consistent energies, forces, and responses across the resulting state space. 
Architectural encoding of physical scaling laws can complement explicit state variables. A 
useful research direction is to test whether similarly economical representations can incorporate 
polarization or magnetic interactions while retaining the required boundary -condition 
dependence. Any gains in parameter efficiency or transferability should be demonstrated 
against matched baselines and the relevant dynamical observables. 
Beyond atomistic observables, MLIPs can supply defect energetics, mobilities, and constitutive 
information to phase -field, continuum, or finite -element models. Metallic deformation and 
fracture studies offer concrete routes to this scale bridging. The transferred quantities inherit 
reference, fit, sampling, and finite -size errors, as well as coarse -graining assumptions. 
Propagating those uncertainties is essential when atomistic mechanisms are used to predict 
larger-scale materials behavior.

The diversity of current MLIPs create s opportunities to select models by the physical and 
computational demands of an application, rather than by architectural generation alone. 
Physics-driven specialization extends the practical reach of MLIPs by aligning model design, 
reference data, and de ployment with the demands of materials dynamics. Broad coverage 
establishes a useful starting point, while reliable application requires suitable physical variables, 
reference fidelity, configurational coverage, conservation properties, and evaluation cost . A 
pretrained model that meets these requirements can be used directly. Where a gap remains, 
targeted adaptation should address it and demonstrate a benefit through the intended 
observables and suitable task -compatible comparisons. The four application co ntexts in 
Section 4 and the validation criteria in Section 5 provide a framework for making those 
decisions. 
A useful next step is to make those requirements explicit. Reporting the validated 
configurations, observables, boundary conditions, and computational settings will help connect 
model benchmarks to scientific claims. This would support a more reliable progression from 
electronic-structure reference data to atomistic mechanisms and, where justified, larger -scale 
predictions.
